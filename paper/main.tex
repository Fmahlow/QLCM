\begin{abstract}
Hybrid quantum--classical diffusion models are usually compared with classical
networks of similar size, which leaves open whether the circuit contributes
anything a classical layer of the same shape could not. We test this for latent
denoisers with a 4--8-qubit variational branch on a classical MLP, distilled into
few-step consistency students, on MNIST, Fashion-MNIST, KMNIST and PneumoniaMNIST.
Each control changes one component: an exact classical simulation of the circuit
without entangling gates (separable surrogate), a shape-matched classical branch,
and ablations of the trained branch. In preregistered sweeps over backbone width,
qubits, depth and data re-uploading, the circuit beat the classical branch by the
preregistered criterion in 1 of 23 cells and entanglement beat the surrogate in
none; with six seeds per cell entanglement passed in 0 of 12 cells, and with a
two-qubit correlator readout in 1 of 8. Pooled over cells, hybrid and surrogate
students differ by $+1.0\%$ (90\% CI over seeds $[-3.1\%, +5.2\%]$) and remain
equivalent with 5{,}000 samples and latent-space metrics, although the trained
circuits are far from separable. Perturbing the branch is detected where it carries
most of the model ($h\le4$), so the null result is not an insensitive metric. With
a wide backbone the branch acts mostly as a learned offset. Distillation works with
dataset-dependent quality: the one-step student matches or beats the 32-step
teacher on three datasets and is 9--13\% worse on MNIST, and it is better than or
equivalent to DPM-Solver++ with four evaluations on three datasets (MNIST
inconclusive). These results bound the circuit's contribution in one small model
family under exact simulation.
\end{abstract}

\textbf{Keywords:} quantum machine learning; consistency distillation; latent
diffusion; benchmarking; classical surrogates; negative results

\section{Introduction}

Diffusion models generate data by reversing a gradual noising process
\citep{ho2020ddpm}, at the cost of many sequential network evaluations.
Latent diffusion moves this process into the latent space of an autoencoder
\citep{rombach2022ldm}. Fast samplers and distillation reduce the number of
evaluations: higher-order ODE solvers \citep{lu2022dpmsolver}, progressive
distillation \citep{salimans2022progressive}, direct endpoint regression
\citep{luhman2021knowledge}, and consistency models, which learn a map from any
point of a sampling trajectory to its clean endpoint
\citep{song2023consistency,song2024improved,luo2023lcm}.

The same two ideas appear in quantum generative modelling. Several recent
models place variational circuits inside the denoiser of a (latent) diffusion
model and report better generated images than their classical baselines
\citep{defalco2024efficient,defalco2025qldm,yeteraydeniz2025medical}; others run
quantum diffusion in an autoencoder's latent space \citep{qian2026hybrid} or
reduce the number of circuit calls with consistency-style or unitary single-step
samplers \citep{kolle2024qddm,zhang2025oqiddm}. On hardware, the number of calls
matters more than for a GPU network, because every call costs a full set of
measurement shots. Distilling a hybrid teacher into a few-step student is
therefore an appealing way to cut the quantum cost of sampling.

Two questions are usually conflated in this literature. The first is whether
the hybrid model works; the second is whether it works \emph{because of the
circuit}. Parameter-matched classical baselines answer the first but not the
second, since they differ from the hybrid model in architecture, not only in
the circuit. Benchmarking studies of quantum classifiers have found that
simple classical models often match or beat them and that removing
entanglement often does not hurt \citep{bowles2024better}, and many
re-uploading models admit efficient classical surrogates
\citep{schreiber2023surrogates,landman2023rff}. Whether this also holds for
hybrid diffusion models has, to our knowledge, not been tested with controls that
isolate the circuit.

We study it in the distillation setting described above. Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A hybrid latent consistency-distillation pipeline in which both the
  teacher and the student can contain a variational branch, with accounting of
  circuit evaluations, timing and finite-shot cost (Section~\ref{sec:method}).
  \item A set of controls that change one component at a time: an exact classical
  simulation of the same circuit without entangling gates, a shape-matched
  classical branch, fully classical networks, and ablations that remove either
  the whole branch or only its input-dependent part (Section~\ref{sec:controls}).
  \item A check of the evaluation itself: floors, positive controls at the level of
  the branch and between independently trained models, 5{,}000-sample estimates,
  precision/recall and latent-space metrics (Section~\ref{sec:res-eval}).
  \item Preregistered sweeps over backbone width, qubit count, depth and data
  re-uploading, repeated with twice the seeds, with a correlator readout and with
  5{,}000 samples, analysed with the preregistered per-cell criteria and with pooled
  equivalence intervals and a power analysis (Section~\ref{sec:res-circuit}).
  \item Evidence that distillation transfers across four datasets, that its MNIST
  gap replicates on new seeds (the preregistered within-10\% criterion is not met
  there), and how it compares with higher-order solvers and direct endpoint
  regression at the same number of network evaluations
  (Section~\ref{sec:res-distillation}).
\end{enumerate}

We make no claim of quantum advantage, and our circuits (4--8 qubits) are
trivially simulable. The question is narrower and, we think, prior to any
advantage claim: whether, in this model family, the circuit changes the
function the model learns by an amount we can detect.

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Diffusion, distillation and consistency models.}
DDIM turns a trained diffusion model into a deterministic sampler that can skip
steps \citep{song2021ddim}; DPM-Solver and its successors reach good quality in
10--20 evaluations with higher-order solvers \citep{lu2022dpmsolver}. Knowledge
distillation into a one-step generator \citep{luhman2021knowledge} and
progressive distillation \citep{salimans2022progressive}, which also introduced
the $v$-parameterisation we use for the teacher, train students to imitate the
teacher's sampler. Consistency distillation trains a student to give the same
output at adjacent points of the teacher's trajectory, with an
exponential-moving-average (EMA) target and a boundary condition at $t=0$
\citep{song2023consistency}; improved techniques refine the training of
consistency models \citep{song2024improved}, and latent consistency models apply
the idea in the latent space of a pretrained latent diffusion model
\citep{luo2023lcm}. We follow the original consistency-distillation recipe in a
small latent space with a cosine noise schedule \citep{nichol2021improved}.

\paragraph{Quantum and hybrid diffusion models.}
QuDDPM and its mixed-state extension define diffusion over quantum states
\citep{zhang2023quddpm,kwun2024mixedquddpm}. For classical images,
\citet{kolle2024qddm} propose quantum denoising diffusion models and a unitary
single-sampling variant inspired by consistency models;
\citet{zhang2025oqiddm} combine optimized quantum implicit neural networks with
consistency models for $O(1)$-step sampling. We could only access the abstract
and a partial preview of this work, so we cannot compare against its training
objective. In latent space, \citet{defalco2025qldm} use an autoencoder with a
10-dimensional latent and variational circuits for image, time and their
combination, \citet{yeteraydeniz2025medical} use quantum residual blocks for
medical images, and \citet{qian2026hybrid} run a mixed-state quantum diffusion
model in an autoencoder's latent space. To our knowledge, none of the works whose
full text we could read distils a hybrid teacher into a few-step student, and
none includes a control that keeps the architecture fixed and removes only the
circuit or only its entanglement.

\paragraph{Benchmarking and dequantisation.}
\citet{bowles2024better} benchmark twelve quantum classifiers against simple
classical models and find that the classical models usually win and that
removing entanglement often does not hurt. \citet{schreiber2023surrogates} show
that large classes of re-uploading models have efficiently computable classical
surrogates, and random-Fourier-feature models approximate many variational
models classically \citep{landman2023rff}. More broadly, the role of advantage as
the goal of quantum machine learning \citep{schuld2022advantage}, classical
shadows of quantum models \citep{jerbi2024shadows}, and the link between
trainability and classical simulability \citep{cerezo2023simulability} have been
examined. The function computed by a circuit with angle encoding is a truncated
Fourier series in the inputs whose frequencies are set by the encoding, and
repeating the encoding (data re-uploading) enlarges the frequency spectrum
\citep{schuld2021encoding}; re-uploading also makes even single-qubit
classifiers universal \citep{perezsalinas2020reuploading}. Our separable
surrogate is an exact classical surrogate: without entangling gates, the circuit
factorises into independent single-qubit rotations of Bloch vectors.

\subsection{Latent space and noise schedule}

A convolutional autoencoder (two stride-2 convolutions, a linear layer to a
16-dimensional latent, and the mirror decoder) is trained for 20 epochs with a
pixel MSE loss on 10{,}000 training images (4{,}708 for PneumoniaMNIST, its full
training split). Latents are standardised with the per-dimension training mean
and standard deviation. We use $T=32$ discrete noise levels with a cosine
schedule \citep{nichol2021improved},
\begin{equation}
  \abar_t = \max\!\left(\cos^2\!\frac{\pi t}{2T},\,10^{-6}\right),\quad \abar_0=1,\qquad
  z_t=\sqrt{\abar_t}\,z_0+\sqrt{1-\abar_t}\,\epsilon,\ \ \epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I),
\end{equation}
so that $z_T$ is essentially pure noise and sampling starts from $\mathcal{N}(0,I)$.

\subsection{Network and branch}
\label{sec:network}

Every teacher and student uses the network of Figure~\ref{fig:arch}. With $u=t/T$,
the input features are $\phi=[z_t,\,u,\,\sin\pi u,\,\cos\pi u]$. The output
is the sum of a classical backbone (a three-layer MLP with SiLU activations
and hidden width $h$; $h=128$ unless stated) and a branch
\begin{equation}
  B(\phi)=W_r\,g\!\left(\pi\tanh(W_c\phi+b_c)\right)+b_r,
\end{equation}
where the compressor produces $2q$ angles $(\theta,\varphi)$ for $q$ qubits,
$g:\mathbb{R}^{2q}\to[-1,1]^q$ is the branch block, and $(W_r,b_r)$ is a linear
readout to the 16-dimensional latent. With $h=128$ and $q=4$, the branch has 256
of the network's 21{,}392 parameters (1.2\%).

\paragraph{Circuit.} The hybrid branch block prepares the product state
$\bigotimes_j R_Z(\varphi_j)R_Y(\theta_j)|0\rangle$, applies $L$ trainable layers
$U_\ell=\mathrm{CZ}_{\mathrm{ring}}\bigotimes_j R_Z(w_{\ell j2})R_Y(w_{\ell j1})$,
where $\mathrm{CZ}_{\mathrm{ring}}$ applies CZ between neighbouring qubits on a
ring, then a fixed $\bigotimes_j R_Y(\pi/4)$, and returns the exact expectation
values $\langle Z_j\rangle$. The final rotation makes phases and entanglement
visible to a $Z$ readout; without it, the last layer's $R_Z$ and CZ gates would
not affect the output. The circuit has $2qL$ trainable parameters (16 for
$q=4$, $L=2$), initialised from $\mathcal{N}(0,0.1^2)$, i.e.\ near the identity.
Because all CZ gates are diagonal and commute, $\mathrm{CZ}_{\mathrm{ring}}^2=I$:
without re-uploading and with even $L$, the entangled circuit at zero trainable
rotations is exactly the circuit without CZ gates, so it starts close to the
separable function (relative to the output's spread, the entangling part is 3--22\%
at initialisation in our runs; Section~\ref{sec:res-e9}). The separable surrogate
is initialised with the same draw. With data re-uploading, the encoding rotations
are repeated before every trainable layer, and the cancellation does not occur. All circuits are simulated exactly as state vectors; the
data-independent gates of each forward pass are fused into one matrix, which is
tested against gate-by-gate simulation.

\subsection{Teacher}

The teacher predicts $v=\sqrt{\abar_t}\,\epsilon-\sqrt{1-\abar_t}\,z_0$
\citep{salimans2022progressive} and is trained with an MSE loss for 150 epochs
(AdamW \citep{loshchilov2019adamw} with PyTorch defaults, i.e.\ weight decay 0.01,
learning rate $10^{-3}$ without schedule, batch 64). It samples with deterministic
DDIM \citep{song2021ddim} on $k$ uniformly spaced levels between $T$ and $1$,
followed by a final step to $t=0$.

\subsection{Consistency distillation}

The student wraps the network in a parameterisation that enforces the
boundary condition $f_\theta(z,0)=z$:
\begin{equation}
  f_\theta(z_t,t)=\abar_t\,z_t+\sqrt{1-\abar_t}\,F_\theta(z_t,t).
  \label{eq:student}
\end{equation}
For a clean latent $z_0$, a level $t\sim\mathcal{U}\{1,\dots,T\}$ and noise
$\epsilon$, let $\hat z_{t-1}=\Phi(z_t;t\to t-1)$ be one DDIM step of the frozen
teacher. The loss is
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}\left\|f_\theta(z_t,t)-f_{\theta^-}(\hat z_{t-1},t-1)\right\|_2^2,
\end{equation}
where $\theta^-$ is an EMA of $\theta$ with decay $\mu$, updated after every
optimiser step \citep{song2023consistency}; the optimiser is as for the teacher.
Unless stated otherwise, students train for 200 epochs with $\mu=0.95$, a budget
chosen after the pilot (Section~\ref{sec:res-budget}). Students of different
kinds trained from the same teacher share the initialisation seed and the exact
sequence of minibatches and noise draws. All student evaluations use the EMA
weights $\theta^-$. One-step sampling returns $f_{\theta^-}(z_T,T)$. With $k$
steps, the student's prediction at each level is re-noised to the next level with
fresh noise before the next evaluation.

\subsection{Controls}
\label{sec:controls}

Each control changes one thing about the network of Section~\ref{sec:network}
and leaves everything else (data, autoencoder, teacher, seeds, minibatch order)
fixed.
\begin{description}
  \item[No entanglement.] The same circuit without CZ gates.
  \item[Separable surrogate.] An exact classical implementation of the
  no-entanglement circuit: each qubit is a Bloch vector rotated by the same
  $R_Y$ and $R_Z$ angles, and the output is $(b_z-b_x)/\sqrt2$, which equals
  $\langle Z\rangle$ after the final $R_Y(\pi/4)$. It computes the same function,
  with the same parameters and gradients, as the no-entanglement circuit, at cost
  linear in $q$. Unit tests confirm that it matches the no-entanglement circuit
  and differs from the entangled one on random inputs.
  \item[Classical branch.] The branch block is replaced by
  $\tanh(\mathrm{Linear}(2q\to q))$: same input, same output dimension and range,
  with cross-feature interactions (36 parameters for $q=4$, against the
  circuit's 16, which favours the control).
  \item[Classical.] No branch at all; only the backbone.
  \item[Correlator readout (E9).] The circuit returns $\langle Z_j\rangle$ and all
  $\langle Z_iZ_j\rangle$, $i<j$ ($q+q(q-1)/2$ features; 10 for $q=4$), so that
  two-qubit correlations, which a product state cannot produce independently of
  its marginals, reach the readout directly. The surrogate returns
  $\langle Z_i\rangle\langle Z_j\rangle$, which is exact without entanglement,
  and the classical branch widens to $\tanh(\mathrm{Linear}(2q\to q+q(q-1)/2))$.
  Unit tests confirm that the correlators of the circuit without CZ gates factorise
  and that those of the entangled circuit do not.
  \item[Branch ablations at evaluation.] In a trained model, either zero the
  readout (\texttt{readout\_zeroed}: removes the branch, including its learned
  bias), or replace the block's output $g$ by its mean over 2{,}048 noisy training
  latents at all noise levels (\texttt{features\_mean}). The latter removes the
  branch's dependence on both $z_t$ and $t$ but keeps its average output.
\end{description}

\subsection{Cost accounting}

Each sampling step evaluates the network once per sample, so a $k$-step
sampler with a circuit makes $k$ circuit evaluations per sample. We report
end-to-end wall-clock time for 512 samples, including the decoder, as the median
of five synchronised repetitions after two warm-up runs on one RTX~3080~Ti.
Simulated circuit time is not a proxy for hardware latency; for hardware cost
we report total measurement shots per sample (steps $\times$ shots per
evaluation) in Section~\ref{sec:res-shots}.

\section{Experimental Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

\paragraph{Datasets.} MNIST \citep{lecun1998mnist}, Fashion-MNIST
\citep{xiao2017fashion}, KMNIST \citep{clanuwat2018kmnist} and PneumoniaMNIST
from MedMNIST v2 \citep{yang2023medmnist} (two unbalanced classes,
1{,}214/3{,}494 training images; CC BY 4.0), all $28\times28$ greyscale.

\paragraph{Metric.} Following \citet{heusel2017fid}, we compute the Fréchet
distance between Gaussian fits to features of generated samples and of real
test images. The features are the 64-dimensional penultimate layer of a small CNN
classifier trained separately for each dataset, not Inception. We call this score
\fidlike{}; lower is better. It is only comparable within a dataset and at a fixed
sample size, never across datasets or to published FID values
\citep{chong2020effectively}. The sweeps and the original campaign use 512
generated samples against the first 1{,}000 test images (all 624 for
PneumoniaMNIST). The classifiers were trained with budgets fixed before inspecting
the test split and reach held-out accuracies of 0.959 (MNIST), 0.889
(Fashion-MNIST), 0.931 (KMNIST) and 0.827 (PneumoniaMNIST); the classifiers
retrained for the review experiments agree within 0.002. We also record the
entropy of the classifier's predicted labels as a diversity check
(Appendix~\ref{app:extra}, Table~\ref{tab:entropy}).

\paragraph{Robustness evaluation.} The review experiments (E6--E8, R1--R4 below)
use 5{,}000 generated samples per model, from one initial noise per dataset and
seed that all models share, against 5{,}000 real test images (all 624 for
PneumoniaMNIST). Besides \fidlike{}, we report precision and recall with $k=3$
nearest neighbours in the same classifier features \citep{kynkaanniemi2019improved},
which separate fidelity from coverage (for PneumoniaMNIST, averaged over ten
random subsets of 624 generated samples so that both sets have the same size),
and two metrics that bypass both the decoder and the classifier: the Fréchet
distance and the unbiased squared MMD with a Gaussian kernel (bandwidth: median
pairwise distance of the reference) \citep{gretton2012kernel} between generated
standardised latents and the latents of the reference images encoded by the same
autoencoder. \emph{Floors.} Generators are trained on the training split, so the
reference floor is real images from that split that no model saw (training images
10{,}000--15{,}000; for PneumoniaMNIST, whose training split is used in full, the
524 validation images) and their autoencoder reconstructions. This rule was fixed
after the first evaluation (R4 below); the preregistered floor, 5{,}000 other test
images, is reported alongside. On MNIST and KMNIST it differs, because the halves
of the test set, and on KMNIST the test and training splits, are not exchangeable.

\paragraph{Teacher gate.} Before training students, each 32-step teacher had
to reach a label entropy of at least $0.8\ln 10$ (for PneumoniaMNIST, $0.8$
times the entropy of the training labels) and a mean classifier confidence of at
least 0.8 times that on real images. All 36 teachers of the campaign, and all 15
of the replication, passed.

\paragraph{Seeds and runs.} Unless stated otherwise, every configuration uses
seeds 0, 1, 2; the replication (E5) uses seeds 3--7 and the extended capacity sweep
(E10) seeds 3--5. The seed fixes the autoencoder, teacher and student, so each
seed is an independent replicate of the whole pipeline. Paired comparisons match
seeds.

\paragraph{Stages, and what was fixed in advance.} The work proceeded in
five stages, each with its own entry in the repository's protocol.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Pilot} (MNIST, hybrid teacher; students with 50 epochs,
  $\mu=0.999$), run under a written protocol with a proposed advance criterion.
  No student met it. We then ran a student-budget diagnostic outside that
  protocol, reusing the pilot's autoencoders and teachers and varying only epochs
  and $\mu$, and selected the student budget on the test-set \fidlike{}
  (Section~\ref{sec:res-budget}).
  \item \emph{Dataset and teacher campaign}: 4 datasets $\times$ 3 seeds; on each
  autoencoder, a hybrid, a classical and a no-entanglement teacher. The hybrid
  teacher is distilled into hybrid, classical and surrogate students; the
  control teachers into a classical student. This stage had no written
  preregistration. For MNIST, the autoencoders, hybrid teachers and their students
  are the runs from the budget diagnostic, so MNIST student results are selected
  on the same metric and seeds.
  \item \emph{Preregistered extension} \textbf{E0--E4}: E0, branch ablations
  (descriptive); E1, a capacity sweep over $h$; E2, circuit scaling with no
  backbone; E3, finite shots and noise at inference (descriptive); E4,
  diagnostics of multi-step degradation and of KMNIST (descriptive).
  \item \emph{Preregistered review experiments} \textbf{E5--E10}, in response to a
  review of an earlier draft: E5, the MNIST campaign on five new seeds with the
  budget of stage 1; E6, a positive control; E7, the robustness evaluation; E8,
  few-step baselines; E9, the correlator readout; E10, the E1 grid with three more
  seeds. The checkpoints of stages 1--3 were not available, so the campaign was
  retrained from scratch with the same code, configuration and seeds on the same
  GPU model. Training turned out to be deterministic: the retrained models give
  the same per-seed label entropies and pixel variances to five or six significant
  digits. The retrained campaign is therefore a re-execution, not an independent
  replication, and its \fidlike{} differs from the original only through the
  retrained classifiers: within 1\% on MNIST, KMNIST and PneumoniaMNIST and 4--5\%
  higher for every model on Fashion-MNIST (Appendix~\ref{app:extra},
  Table~\ref{tab:repro}). The retraining was started three minutes before the
  preregistration was committed; it involves no criterion and produced no result
  before the commit.
  \item \emph{Review round 1} \textbf{R1--R6}, preregistered after a second review
  and before running: R1, the sweeps of E9 and E10 re-scored with the robustness
  evaluation; R2, the E6 branch perturbations where the branch carries the model;
  R3, a positive control between independently trained models; R4, the floors;
  R5, endpoint regression with more data; R6, robustness analyses of pooled
  intervals.
\end{enumerate}
Sections on stages 1--3 report the original runs. E5--E8 and R1--R5 use the
retrained campaign or new runs. E10 combines the original E1 runs (seeds 0--2,
original classifiers) with new runs (seeds 3--5, retrained classifiers); all its
comparisons are paired within a seed.

\paragraph{Preregistered criteria (E1, E2, E9).} In each cell (dataset and $h$, or
circuit configuration), with teacher and student of the same kind and size and
the one-step student's \fidlike{} as the primary metric: the \emph{circuit
contributes} if the hybrid model beats the classical branch in all three seeds
and its mean is at least 10\% lower; \emph{entanglement contributes} if the
hybrid beats the separable surrogate under the same rule. The 32-step teacher is
the preregistered secondary metric. Because E1 MNIST $h=0$ and E2 with 4 qubits,
depth 2 and no re-uploading are the same configuration, E1 and E2 have 23 unique
cells. E10 adapts the rule to six seeds (at least five better).

\paragraph{Added after the preregistrations.} The following should be read as
exploratory: the \texttt{features\_mean} ablation and the RMS of the branch's
input-varying part in E0; the four-step student as an additional secondary metric
in E1 and E2; all equivalence tests and pooled intervals and the power analysis;
the analyses of the campaign (stage 2); in E6, the contamination and
readout-zeroed controls on the $h=128$ campaign (added after the preregistered
perturbations turned out not to produce degradations of known size); in E7, the
floor rule above; and in E8, two implementation changes made after a smoke test
with one-epoch models and before any real run: the first Heun step is an Euler
step, because $\sigma_t/\alpha_t\approx10^3$ at the terminal level makes the
trapezoidal correction there unstable, and the grid of evaluations changed from
1/2/4/8 (DPM-Solver++) and 3/5/7 (Heun) to 3/4/6/8 and 4/6/8, because
DPM-Solver++(2M) with one or two evaluations is DDIM and Heun with $k$ steps then
costs $2k-2$ evaluations. That change was first written into the preregistration
text in place; the protocol now restores the original text and records it as a
dated amendment.

\paragraph{Equivalence analysis.} The preregistered criteria can detect a
contribution but cannot establish its absence. We therefore use two one-sided
tests (TOST) on paired log-ratios of the metric \citep{lakens2017tost}: for each
pair of models with the same seed (and dataset), $r=\log(m_A/m_B)$. We report the
geometric-mean ratio with a 90\% $t$-interval and call two models
\emph{equivalent} only if the interval lies inside $[1/1.1,\,1.1]$, the same 10\%
used by the preregistered criteria; we say ``significant'' when the interval
excludes 1. Intervals are computed over all paired runs, and with the cell (or
dataset) as the unit, i.e.\ a $t$-interval over per-cell mean log-ratios. Because
all cells of a seed share an autoencoder, pooled sweep intervals are also computed
with the seed as the unit: the mean log-ratio over cells for each seed, and a
$t$-interval over seeds (R6; this replaces a percentile bootstrap over seeds used in
an earlier draft, which is anti-conservative with three to six seeds). Where the two
differ, claims rest on the wider one. Tables of \fidlike{} report arithmetic means; ratios are geometric
means of paired ratios unless stated.

\paragraph{Floors.}
\label{sec:res-e7}

\paragraph{Perturbing the branch.}
\label{sec:res-e6}
E6 perturbed the trained branch of the $h=128$ campaign models and asked whether
the change is detected (preregistered rule: 90\% interval of the paired ratio over
three seeds excludes 1 and the change is at least 10\%). It is not: scaling the
input-dependent part of the branch output by $a\in\{0,0.5,1.5,2,3\}$ or permuting it
across samples moves \fidlike{} by $-9\%$ to $+7\%$ (Appendix
Table~\ref{tab:e6full}). This only confirms that at $h=128$ the branch is a small
part of the function (E0, Section~\ref{sec:res-e0}); it does not show that the
evaluation is sensitive. R2 therefore applied the same perturbations where the
branch carries the model: the E10 hybrid pipelines with $h\in\{0,4,16\}$
(Table~\ref{tab:r2}). There 48 of the 84 preregistered perturbations meet the
detection rule (a detected change in either direction). Removing the input-dependent
part ($a=0$) worsens the one-step student by 14\% to 274\% in all six cells, and
zeroing the readout is detected in all 12 cases. Permuting the branch outputs
across samples is detected for the teacher at $h=0$ (both datasets) and MNIST
$h=4$, and for the student at MNIST $h=16$ and Fashion-MNIST $h=4$; for the
one-step student without backbone it only reorders the samples and changes nothing.
Eleven of the detections are \emph{improvements}: amplifying the input-dependent
part by 1.5--3 lowers \fidlike{} by up to 34\% at small $h$, the same preference for
added spread that latent noise shows below. At $h=16$ on Fashion-MNIST only zeroing
the readout is detected for the teacher. The evaluation therefore detects changes of
the branch's function where the branch carries most of the model ($h\le4$), and less
reliably at $h=16$.

\paragraph{Differences between independently trained models.}
The equivalence results compare models trained independently, whose ratios vary
from seed to seed. R3 asks whether the same machinery detects known capacity
differences between such models (Table~\ref{tab:r3}; the pairs MLP $h=32$/$128$
and hybrid $h=16$/$32$ were added after the preregistration). Halving or quartering the
width of the backbone-only MLP changes the one-step \fidlike{} by $+9\%$ to $+78\%$;
with six seeds every one of these differences is significant, with three seeds and
5{,}000 samples all but one (Fashion-MNIST $h=16$ against $32$). Between hybrid
networks, $h=16$ against $h=32$ ($+16$ to $+31\%$) is detected everywhere, and $h=32$
against $h=128$ ($+2$ to $+11\%$) only on Fashion-MNIST with six seeds. Paired comparisons of
independently trained pipelines therefore resolve differences of about 15--20\% or
more with three seeds, not reliably smaller ones (an 18.6\% and an 8.4\% difference
are missed); with six seeds an 8.5\% difference is detected and a 10.7\% one is not. Two further checks are in the
appendix: the \fidlike{} of a fixed model's samples responds to known contamination
(replacing 5\% of them by poor samples raises it by 7--19\%; added post hoc), and it \emph{decreases} when Gaussian noise is added
to the generated latents (by up to 32\% at $s=0.4$), while precision falls and
recall rises. The generated distributions are under-dispersed, and \fidlike{}
rewards added spread, as also seen with finite shots in E3; this is why we also
report precision, recall and latent metrics.

\subsection{Does the circuit contribute?}
\label{sec:res-circuit}

\subsubsection{E1 and E2: no regime in which the circuit contributes detectably}
\label{sec:res-sweeps}

The preregistered sweeps are the most direct test of the circuit, because they
include regimes in which the branch carries most or all of the model. E1 shrinks
the backbone down to removing it ($h=0$; Figure~\ref{fig:capacity}); E2 scales the
circuit with no backbone: 4, 6 and 8 qubits, depth 2 and 4, with and without data
re-uploading (per-cell tables in Appendix~\ref{app:extra},
Tables~\ref{tab:e1} and~\ref{tab:e2}).

\paragraph{Preregistered criteria.} On the primary metric, the circuit criterion
is met in 1 of 23 unique cells and the entanglement criterion in none. The single
pass is MNIST with $h=16$ (hybrid 11\% better than the classical branch); there the
separable surrogate is close to the hybrid (per seed 35.2/30.4/46.2 against
35.2/30.5/44.0). On the preregistered secondary metric (32-step teacher) the circuit
criterion passes in four cells and the entanglement criterion never. These secondary
passes are scattered, with no pattern in $h$, dataset or qubit count, and are
uncorrected for multiple comparisons.

\paragraph{Power of the criteria.} The criteria detect only large effects. From
the paired seed-to-seed spread of the log-ratios (median SD 0.10), a simulation of
the rule gives a per-cell probability of passing of 0.43 for a true 10\%
improvement, 0.76 for 15\% and 0.95 for 20\%, and a false-pass rate of 3--10\%
when there is no effect (Appendix~\ref{app:extra}, Table~\ref{tab:power}). One pass
in 23 cells is therefore consistent with no effect, and single cells cannot
exclude improvements of 10--15\%; the pooled intervals are the stronger evidence.

\paragraph{Pooled intervals.} Pooling cells, with the cell as the unit
(Table~\ref{tab:sweeppooled}): over the 12 cells of E1, hybrid and surrogate
students differ by $-0.0\%$ ($[-2.9\%,+2.9\%]$; with the three seeds as units
$[-5.3\%,+5.5\%]$), and so do the six E2 configurations without re-uploading
($+2.0\%$, $[-2.0\%,+6.1\%]$). With re-uploading, entanglement makes the student
significantly worse: $+16.9\%$ ($[+0.2\%,+36.4\%]$). Against the classical branch,
the hybrid is $-4.0\%$ ($[-9.3\%,+1.5\%]$) at $h\ge16$ and $+14.1\%$
($[-1.7\%,+32.4\%]$) at $h\le8$.

\paragraph{When the circuit carries the model, it tends to hurt.} With no or a
tiny backbone on Fashion-MNIST, the classical branch is much better than the
circuit (ratio of means 1.54 at $h=0$ and 1.31 at $h=4$). In E2, data re-uploading
makes every one-step hybrid student worse than the classical branch (ratios of
means 1.43--2.86) and much more variable across seeds. Without re-uploading, more
qubits help (one-step \fidlike{} 144, 86, 52 for 4, 6, 8 qubits at depth 2), but a
classical branch of the same width improves similarly (139, 73, 55). At $h=0$ the
output is an affine function of the $q$ branch outputs, so its rank is at most $q$;
the gain with $q$ is consistent with this readout rank and with the width of the
compressor ($2q$ angles), which the classical branch shares.

\paragraph{Parameter frontier.} Rows with the same $h$ do not have the same size.
The preregistered frontier compares each hybrid network with the best
backbone-only MLP that has at most as many parameters (Figure~\ref{fig:capacity};
Appendix Table~\ref{tab:frontier}). At the two smallest hybrid sizes (256 and 436
parameters) the MLP is better in three of four cases; from 632 parameters upward the
hybrid is better or within seed noise, and the largest gap, Fashion-MNIST at $h=8$
(70.7 against 86.0), is matched by the classical branch (67.9). A
parameter-matched comparison therefore does not favour the hybrid model either.

\paragraph{Distillation needs a backbone.} With $h=0$, the one-step students score
131--157 (99 for the Fashion-MNIST classical branch) against 52--57 for the
32-step teachers. We attribute this, without a direct test, to insufficient
capacity for the one-step map.

\subsubsection{E9: a correlator readout does not make entanglement matter}
\label{sec:res-e9}

With a single-qubit readout, entanglement can reach the output only through local
marginals. E9 gives the readout all two-qubit correlators
(Section~\ref{sec:controls}) in four cells per dataset ($q=4$, depth 2 with
$h\in\{0,4,16\}$, and $q=6$, depth 4 with $h=0$), with the single-qubit readout in the
same run as a matched reference (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:e9}). Preregistered rules,
as in E1: \emph{entanglement contributes} (hybrid against surrogate, both with
correlators), \emph{the circuit contributes} (against the classical branch of the
same output width), and \emph{correlators help} (hybrid with against without). On the
primary metric the entanglement rule is met once in eight cells (MNIST, $h=0$,
$q=4$: ratio 0.85, with an unusually variable surrogate, $156\pm27$), the circuit
rule never, and the correlator rule never. On the 32-step teacher the entanglement
rule never passes, the circuit rule passes in three Fashion-MNIST cells and the
correlator rule in four cells. Pooled over the eight cells
(Table~\ref{tab:e9pooled}), hybrid and surrogate students are equivalent
($-0.8\%$, $[-8.3\%,+7.3\%]$), and the correlators do not detectably change either
student ($+1.5\%$ and $+1.6\%$). They make the hybrid teacher 6.2\% better
($[-10.7\%,-1.6\%]$); with correlators, hybrid and surrogate teachers differ by
$-2.4\%$ ($[-4.0\%,-0.8\%]$), a significant but small effect of entanglement, inside
the margin. With correlators the classical branch is much better than the circuit
(pooled $+62\%$ for the hybrid, driven by the cells with little or no backbone). It
also has many more parameters (90 against 16 for $q=4$), and we have not tested
whether its advantage comes from that or from the wider learned nonlinearity.

\subsubsection{Is the entanglement null an initialisation artefact?}
\label{sec:res-entangling}

Without re-uploading and with even depth the entangled circuit is initialised at
(nearly) the separable function (Section~\ref{sec:network}), and hybrid and surrogate
share their initial parameters. The equivalence of the two could therefore reflect an
optimiser that never leaves the separable neighbourhood. We checked this after the
second review (not preregistered): for every trained hybrid pipeline of E9 and E10,
we evaluated the circuit with and without its CZ gates, at the same weights, on its
actual input angles (the compressor applied to noised training latents at all
levels), and took the RMS difference relative to the spread of the circuit's
features (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:entangling}). At initialisation this
\emph{entangling contribution} is 0.03--0.22; after training it is 0.06--0.79 in the
teachers and 0.02--2.09 in the students (median 0.57), so most trained circuits are
far from separable. Across the 84 student pipelines it is unrelated to the
hybrid/surrogate ratio (Spearman $\rho=-0.03$, $p=0.76$;
Figure~\ref{fig:entangling}). Entanglement is functionally present in the trained
circuits, often strongly, and still does not change the quality of the generator.
Re-uploading, where the cancellation does not occur, is also the one setting in which
entanglement made results significantly worse (E2).

\subsubsection{R1: the sweeps with 5{,}000 samples and other metrics}
\label{sec:res-r1}

The circuit question is decided in the sweeps, which use 512 samples. R1 re-scored
the existing E9 (seeds 0--2) and E10 (seeds 3--5) checkpoints with the robustness
evaluation (Table~\ref{tab:r1}; E9 with single-qubit and correlator readout reported
separately, since their classical branches differ). The preregistered robustness
criterion was that the pooled hybrid/surrogate comparisons stay equivalent in
\fidlike{} and in the latent Fréchet distance. They do, for students and teachers,
with all intervals inside $\pm10\%$ (student \fidlike{}: $+1.9\%$ in E10, $-1.1\%$
and $-0.8\%$ in E9 with single-qubit and correlator readout). Three intervals exclude

in the latent Fréchet distance; with correlators the hybrid student is
$1.8\times10^{-3}$ worse in latent MMD (against values of 7--14$\times10^{-3}$) and the
hybrid teacher 2.3\% better in \fidlike{}. The per-cell rules at 5{,}000 samples give, on the student,
one entanglement pass in each sweep (E9 MNIST $h=0$ with correlators, the same cell
as at 512 samples; E10 MNIST $h=128$, which did not pass at 512 samples or with six

branch at $h\ge16$, the hybrid student's advantage shrinks to $-4.7\%$
($[-11.5\%,+2.6\%]$) and is not seen in the latent metric ($-1.3\%$). The
\emph{entanglement} conclusion does not depend on the sample size or the metric.

\subsubsection{The campaign: hybrid and classical models are equivalent at $h=128$}
\label{sec:res-campaign-eq}
\label{sec:res-equivalence}

In the campaign, teachers with a circuit, with a circuit without entanglement, and
without any branch have nearly identical DDIM curves on all datasets, and the same
holds for the students of the hybrid teacher (Appendix Tables~\ref{tab:campaign}
and~\ref{tab:students}). With 512 samples every comparison is equivalent within
$\pm10\%$, with dataset-level intervals within $\pm4\%$ (Appendix
Table~\ref{tab:equivalence}). With 5{,}000 samples (Table~\ref{tab:e7}) they remain
equivalent: the largest interval bound is 2.1\% over runs and 3.2\% over datasets
in \fidlike{} and 4.9\% in the latent Fréchet distance, latent MMD differs by at most
$0.13\times10^{-3}$ on average (against values of order $10^{-2}$), and precision and
recall by less than half a percentage point. The intervals are about as wide as with 512 samples (0.8--1.8 times
narrower), so their width is set by the variation between independently trained
pipelines, not by the number of samples. A few intervals exclude 1 while staying
inside the margin: the hybrid student is 0.9\% better than the classical student
(which has no branch and fewer parameters), the hybrid teacher 1.0\% worse than the
no-entanglement teacher in the latent Fréchet distance (and $0.06\times10^{-3}$ higher
in latent MMD), and on the new MNIST seeds
the hybrid teacher 2.5\% better than the classical teacher when pooled over eight
seeds (Section~\ref{sec:res-e5}). This regime is, however, the least informative about the circuit: the
branch is 1.2\% of the parameters and its input-dependent output is small (E0).

\subsection{Does distillation of a hybrid teacher work?}
\label{sec:res-distillation}

\subsubsection{Student budget}
\label{sec:res-budget}

In the pilot, no student met the advance criterion we had proposed (in every
seed, \fidlike{} at most 10\% worse than the 32-step teacher, label entropy loss
at most $0.1\ln10$, and at least $2\times$ faster): the best one-step students
scored 347.5 (hybrid) and 426.9 (classical), against 24.7 for the 32-step teacher
(Appendix Table~\ref{tab:budget}). Changing only the student's epochs and EMA decay
resolved the failure: lowering $\mu$ to 0.95, or training four times longer, brings
the one-step \fidlike{} to 28--32. We hypothesise, without having tested it, that
with $\mu=0.999$ and 50 epochs the slowly moving target does not propagate the
boundary condition up to $t=T$. We use 200 epochs and $\mu=0.95$ for all later
students. Because this budget was selected on the test metric, E5 replicates the
MNIST campaign on new seeds.

\subsubsection{Distillation across datasets}
\label{sec:res-distill}

Figure~\ref{fig:steps} and Table~\ref{tab:studteach} show the campaign ($h=128$,
4 qubits; samples in Figure~\ref{fig:samples}). Few-step students are far ahead of
the teacher run with one to four DDIM steps on all four datasets. How close the
one-step student comes to the full 32-step teacher depends on the dataset: within
10\% in every seed on Fashion-MNIST and KMNIST, better than the teacher on
PneumoniaMNIST, and 13\% worse on MNIST (within 10\% in one of three seeds). With
5{,}000 samples (Table~\ref{tab:e7}) the one-step student is $+13.1\%$ worse on MNIST
($[+5.1\%,+21.7\%]$), $+2.1\%$ on KMNIST, and better on Fashion-MNIST ($-2.4\%$) and
PneumoniaMNIST ($-18.5\%$); pooled, it trades about four percentage points of
precision for five of recall. On the three original MNIST seeds a four-step student
met the advance criterion in every seed ($-2.2\%$), but this does not hold on new
seeds (Section~\ref{sec:res-e5}). Students do not improve monotonically with steps:
on MNIST and especially PneumoniaMNIST, quality degrades beyond 4--8 steps, and the
four-step student is $+42\%$ worse than the teacher in the latent Fréchet distance
while not in \fidlike{}; Section~\ref{sec:res-e4} traces this to a fraction of
diverging samples, which the decoder and classifier compress.

\subsubsection{Cost of sampling}
\label{sec:res-cost}

Per sampling step, models with a simulated circuit are about 8 times slower than
classical ones at one step (10.8 against 1.3~ms for 512 samples) and about 13 times
slower for the 32-step teacher (329 against 26~ms). Simulating the circuit without
entanglement costs the same (329~ms), whereas the separable surrogate computes that
function 5--6 times faster: 2.2 against 10.8~ms for one-step students, and 54~ms for
the 32-step surrogate student against 329~ms for the 32-step hybrid teacher, both
networks of the same size.



\subsubsection{E8: distillation against higher-order solvers and endpoint regression}
\label{sec:res-e8}

DDIM is a first-order solver, so the gap to the student overstates what
distillation buys. E8 evaluates the hybrid teacher with DPM-Solver++(2M)
\citep{lu2022dpmsolverpp} and Heun's method \citep{karras2022edm} on the same
integer levels as DDIM, and adds direct endpoint regression
\citep{luhman2021knowledge}: a classical network of the student's size trained for
200 epochs to map fixed Gaussian latents to the endpoints of the teacher's 32-step
DDIM trajectories (Figure~\ref{fig:solvers}; Appendix Table~\ref{tab:e8}).
Higher-order solvers help a lot: with six evaluations DPM-Solver++ comes within 1\%
of the 32-step DDIM teacher or beats it on every dataset. The preregistered question
was whether the one-step student is better than the best solver with at most four
evaluations (Table~\ref{tab:e8ratios}). It is much better on Fashion-MNIST
($-32.8\%$) and PneumoniaMNIST ($-66.1\%$), equivalent on KMNIST ($-1.1\%$,
$[-4.0\%,+2.0\%]$), and inconclusive on MNIST ($+4.6\%$, $[-12.0\%,+24.3\%]$). Against
DPM-Solver++ with eight evaluations the student is worse on MNIST ($+17.4\%$) and
KMNIST ($+10.5\%$), equal on Fashion-MNIST and better on PneumoniaMNIST. Endpoint
regression with 10{,}000 pairs ties the consistency student on MNIST and KMNIST and is
worse on Fashion-MNIST ($+9.4\%$) and PneumoniaMNIST ($+23.8\%$). With 100{,}000 pairs
(R5; about ten times the consistency student's number of gradient steps, which
favours the baseline), the picture is the same ($+7.0\%$ and $+23.4\%$; $-5.8\%$ and
$+1.3\%$ on MNIST and KMNIST) and its held-out loss equals its training loss, so the
deficit of the 10{,}000-pair model is not explained by too little data. The advantage of consistency distillation over these alternatives
is real but dataset-dependent, and for a circuit-bearing teacher the relevant
reference is a 4--8-evaluation solver, not DDIM.

\subsubsection{E3: shots and noise}
\label{sec:res-shots}

We evaluated noiselessly trained MNIST models with finite shots and local
depolarising noise at inference. Each circuit evaluation is simulated as a
density matrix with depolarising probability $p$ on every qubit after the
encoding and after each trainable layer; all qubits are measured in the
computational basis with $S$ shots per evaluation, and $\langle Z_j\rangle$ are
estimated from the counts. Table~\ref{tab:e3} and Figure~\ref{fig:shots} show
$p\in\{0,0.03\}$ and $S\in\{\text{exact},64\}$; the full grid
($p\in\{0,0.001,0.01,0.03\}$, $S\in\{\text{exact},1024,256,64\}$) is in the
repository.

With the backbone ($h=128$), 64 shots and $p=0.03$ leave \fidlike{} unchanged, as E0
leads one to expect. At an equal total shot budget, the distilled student is better
than the teacher run with fewer DDIM steps: one step with 64 shots (64 shots per
sample) gives 27.9, against 52.2 for the four-step teacher (256 shots per sample);
the 32-step teacher needs 2{,}048 shots per sample to reach 24.7. A higher-order
solver is the stronger reference: on MNIST, the comparison of DPM-Solver++ with four
evaluations and the one-step student is inconclusive (Section~\ref{sec:res-e8}), so
there the saving is at most a factor of four in shots, not the factor implied by the
DDIM comparison;
we did not evaluate solvers under finite shots. Without the backbone, distillation
fails (Section~\ref{sec:res-sweeps}), and noise and shots matter more: $p=0.03$ moves
the one-step student from 144.4 to 158.4 (exact) and the four-step student from
110.7 to 99.7, and 64-shot sampling slightly improves \fidlike{} (144.4 to 138.7),
another instance of the metric rewarding added variance.

\subsubsection{E4: why students degrade with steps, and the KMNIST bottleneck}
\label{sec:res-e4}

\paragraph{Multi-step degradation (E4a).} Measured against the endpoint of the
teacher's full DDIM trajectory from the same $z_t$, the student's error is small
at every $t$ (latent MSE 0.0003 at $t=1$ and 0.027 at $t=32$ on MNIST, against a
signal energy of about 1), also when the input is the student's own re-noised
output. Instead, a growing fraction of samples diverges when the student is applied
repeatedly (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:e4a}): the median latent energy stays near 1,
but the tail explodes, with maxima at 32 steps of $5\times10^2$--$4\times10^3$ on
MNIST and $10^5$--$7\times10^7$ on PneumoniaMNIST. Clipping or rescaling the latent
between steps, or training on the student's own re-noised predictions, are possible
fixes that we did not test. The hybrid and surrogate students behave the same (rows
in the repository's report).

\paragraph{KMNIST is limited by the autoencoder (E4b).} With a 16-dimensional
latent, encoding and decoding real KMNIST test images already gives a \fidlike{} of
204, about the same as generation (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:e4b}). With a
64-dimensional latent, reconstruction falls to 25 and the bottleneck moves to the
denoiser, where distillation still holds (one-step student 124.6 against 113.8 for
the 32-step teacher).

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What the controls rule out.} The similarity of hybrid and classical
models could have several explanations: the optimiser could ignore the branch,
the circuit could compute something useful that a classical branch also computes,
entanglement could matter but too little to detect, or the evaluation could be
blind. The controls separate these. The evaluation detects changes of the branch's
function where the branch carries most of the model, and differences of about 15\%
or more between independently trained models with three seeds (R2, R3). The branch is used (zeroing it hurts) but
at $h=128$ mostly through its average output (E0). A classical layer of the same
shape matches the circuit within the margin or beats it, markedly so when the branch
carries the model (E1, E2, E10). Entanglement never improved on the exact separable
surrogate in a way that was replicated: it met the per-cell criterion at most once
per sweep, in a different cell each time, and pooled over cells the two are equivalent within
a few percent with three or six seeds, 512 or 5{,}000 samples, classifier or latent
metrics, single-qubit or correlator readout, and with data re-uploading
entanglement made the student worse. This is not because the trained circuits stay
separable: most of them are far from it, and how far is unrelated to the
hybrid/surrogate ratio. The only consistent advantage of the circuit
over the classical branch (about 5\% with a wide backbone, inside the margin) is
shared by the separable surrogate. Single cells cannot exclude effects of
10--15\%.

\paragraph{Why entanglement does not help here.} Without re-uploading, each of the
$2q$ angles enters once, so each $\langle Z_j\rangle$ is a low-degree trigonometric
polynomial in those angles \citep{schuld2021encoding}, followed by a learned linear
readout, and the compressor and readout trained jointly with the circuit can absorb
much of what the entangling layers would add. With even depth and no re-uploading
the CZ layers cancel at initialisation, so the circuit starts at the separable
function, but training moves it away (Section~\ref{sec:res-entangling}) without
changing the result, and giving the readout the two-qubit correlators did not make
entanglement detectable either (E9). We did not test a different initialisation, deeper circuits with more
entangling layers, or a readout rank fixed across $q$. Re-uploading enlarges the
frequency spectrum \citep{schuld2021encoding} but, here, mainly made optimisation
harder.

\paragraph{What remains of distillation.} Consistency distillation of a hybrid
teacher reduces the number of circuit evaluations by $8$--$32\times$ relative to the
32-step DDIM teacher, at a quality that depends on the dataset: equal or better on
three datasets and 9--13\% worse on MNIST, on the selection seeds and on new seeds
alike; the four-step MNIST result was optimistic. Against the stronger reference,
a higher-order solver with four evaluations, the one-step student is much better on
two datasets, equivalent on KMNIST and inconclusive on MNIST; endpoint regression
ties it on MNIST and KMNIST and is worse on the other two. This would matter for any
model in which the circuit could not be replaced; in the model family studied here,
it can be.

\paragraph{Implications for benchmarking.} Hybrid generative models are often
compared with classical models that have a similar number of parameters. In our
sweep, comparisons at equal backbone width favoured the hybrid model at small
$h$; at equal parameter count they did not, and a classical branch of the same shape
showed that the circuit was not the reason for the hybrid's advantage over
narrower MLPs. We suggest that claims of a benefit from a circuit be accompanied by
(i) a classical block with the same inputs and outputs in the same place, (ii) an
exact separable surrogate when entanglement is claimed to matter, (iii) an ablation
that replaces the circuit's output by its mean, which separates a learned offset
from an input-dependent computation, (iv) pooled intervals or a power analysis,
since three-seed per-cell comparisons cannot exclude moderate effects, and (v) a
positive control in the regime that is being tested. Our own branch perturbations
failed as a control at $h=128$, where the branch is small, and succeeded at small
$h$; added latent noise even improved \fidlike{}, so a metric's response to a
perturbation has to be checked, not assumed.

\paragraph{Limitations.} The results hold for one architecture: an additive
branch on a classical MLP in a 16-dimensional autoencoder latent, with 4--8
qubits, depth up to 4, angle encoding, a hardware-efficient ansatz and
single-qubit or two-qubit correlator readout. Other placements, encodings,
readouts or ans\"atze could behave differently; in particular, all configurations
without re-uploading have even depth, so the entangled circuit is initialised at the
separable function. Most cells have three seeds (six in
E10; E2 still has three, and its re-uploading cells are the noisiest). The student
budget was selected on the test metric; E5 shows that this did not create the
equivalences and did not inflate the one-step result, but it did inflate the
four-step result on MNIST. The original campaign was not preregistered, several
analyses and controls were added after the corresponding preregistered runs, and
one implementation change was first written into the preregistration in place
(Section~\ref{sec:protocol}). The checkpoints of the original runs were lost; the
retrained campaign re-executes them exactly, but the retrained classifiers move
Fashion-MNIST \fidlike{} by about 5\% for all models, and E10 mixes original and
retrained classifiers across seeds (comparisons are paired within a seed). There is
no correction for multiple comparisons in the per-cell criteria. \fidlike{} uses
features of a small classifier (82.5--95.9\% accuracy) and, for PneumoniaMNIST, at
most 624 real images; it is not comparable to standard FID, rewards added variance
(E3, E6), and on Fashion-MNIST and KMNIST it is dominated by the autoencoder. Noise
was applied only at inference, with a simple local depolarising model and no readout
error or crosstalk; training with shots or noise was not tested, and the solvers were
not evaluated under finite shots. We could not read the full text of the closest
consistency-based quantum diffusion model \citep{zhang2025oqiddm}. Finally, our
circuits are small enough to simulate exactly, so nothing here bears on regimes
where simulation is hard.

\paragraph{Reproducibility and history.} An earlier version of this project
(\texttt{v1/} in the repository) used a classical teacher and an earlier
training procedure. It contained implementation errors, documented in the
repository, and its raw outputs could not be fully recovered; none of its
numbers are used here. Every number in this paper is regenerated from the
committed JSON reports by \texttt{paper/analysis/make\_assets.py}; training and
evaluation code, configurations, the preregistrations (with dated amendments for
changes made after them) and environment records are in \texttt{v2/}, and the
simulated reviews of this draft in \texttt{paper/reviews/}.

\section{Conclusion}

We distilled hybrid quantum latent diffusion teachers into few-step consistency
students and asked, with controls that change one component at a time, whether the
circuit contributes. Across 23 preregistered capacity and scaling cells, the circuit
met the contribution criterion against a shape-matched classical branch once, and
entanglement never beat an exact separable surrogate. With six seeds per cell, with a
two-qubit correlator readout and with 5{,}000 samples, entanglement met the criterion
at most once per sweep and never in the same cell twice, although the trained circuits
are far from separable. Pooled over cells, hybrid and surrogate are equivalent within
a few percent, and with data re-uploading entanglement made results worse. The
evaluation detects changes of the branch where the branch carries most of the model, and
in the trained models with a wide backbone the branch acts mostly as a learned
offset. Distillation transferred across four datasets: one-step students matched or
beat the 32-step teacher on three and were 9--13\% worse on MNIST, and they were
better than or equivalent to a four-evaluation higher-order solver on three
datasets, with MNIST inconclusive. We see this as a negative result for one model
family and as a case for including such controls in hybrid generative benchmarks.
Natural next steps are architectures in which the circuit cannot be bypassed, such
as models of quantum data \citep{zhang2023quddpm}, where distilling a few-step
student would reduce a cost that cannot be avoided classically.
