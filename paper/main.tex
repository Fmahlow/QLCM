\author{Felipe Mahlow\thanks{Manuscript in preparation. Affiliation and acknowledgements to be completed.}}

\begin{abstract}
Hybrid quantum--classical diffusion models for image generation are usually
compared against classical networks with a similar number of parameters, which
leaves open whether the parameterized circuit contributes anything that a
classical layer of the same shape could not. We test this in a setting where it
is also practically relevant: consistency distillation of a hybrid latent
diffusion teacher into a few-step student, which cuts the number of circuit
evaluations per sample. The models are latent denoisers with a 4--8-qubit
variational branch added to a classical MLP, trained on MNIST, Fashion-MNIST,
KMNIST and PneumoniaMNIST with three seeds. Each control changes one component:
an exact, linear-cost classical simulation of the same circuit without
entangling gates (separable surrogate); a classical branch with the same inputs,
outputs and range; and ablations of the trained branch. In preregistered sweeps
over backbone width, qubit count (up to 8), depth and data re-uploading, the
circuit met the contribution criterion against the size-matched classical branch
in 1 of 23 cells and entanglement met it against the surrogate in none. Pooled
over the capacity sweep, hybrid and surrogate students differ by $0.0\%$ in
classifier-feature \fidlike{} (90\% CI $[-2.9\%, +2.9\%]$, cells as units); with
data re-uploading, entanglement made results worse ($+16.9\%$). In the
dataset campaign, hybrid, no-entanglement and classical teachers and students are
equivalent within $\pm4\%$. In trained models the branch acts mostly as a learned
offset. Distillation itself works: the one-step student is within 10\% of the
32-step teacher on three datasets, a four-step student is needed on MNIST, and at
an equal finite-shot budget the distilled student beats the teacher run with fewer
DDIM steps. These results bound the circuit's contribution in one model family at
small scale under exact simulation; they are not claims about quantum circuits in
general.
\end{abstract}

\textbf{Keywords:} quantum machine learning; consistency distillation; latent
diffusion; benchmarking; classical surrogates; negative results

\section{Introduction}

Diffusion models generate data by reversing a gradual noising process
\citep{ho2020ddpm}, at the cost of many sequential network evaluations.
Latent diffusion moves this process into the latent space of an autoencoder
\citep{rombach2022ldm}. Fast samplers and distillation reduce the number of
evaluations: higher-order ODE solvers \citep{lu2022dpmsolver}, progressive
distillation \citep{salimans2022progressive}, direct endpoint regression
\citep{luhman2021knowledge}, and consistency models, which learn a map from any
point of a sampling trajectory to its clean endpoint
\citep{song2023consistency,song2024improved,luo2023lcm}.

The same two ideas appear in quantum generative modelling. Several recent
models place variational circuits inside the denoiser of a (latent) diffusion
model and report better generated images than their classical baselines
\citep{defalco2024efficient,defalco2025qldm,yeteraydeniz2025medical}; others run
quantum diffusion in an autoencoder's latent space \citep{qian2026hybrid} or
reduce the number of circuit calls with consistency-style or unitary single-step
samplers \citep{kolle2024qddm,zhang2025oqiddm}. On hardware, the number of calls
matters more than for a GPU network, because every call costs a full set of
measurement shots. Distilling a hybrid teacher into a few-step student is
therefore an appealing way to cut the quantum cost of sampling.

Two questions are usually conflated in this literature. The first is whether
the hybrid model works; the second is whether it works \emph{because of the
circuit}. Parameter-matched classical baselines answer the first but not the
second, since they differ from the hybrid model in architecture, not only in
the circuit. Benchmarking studies of quantum classifiers have found that
simple classical models often match or beat them and that removing
entanglement often does not hurt \citep{bowles2024better}, and many
re-uploading models admit efficient classical surrogates
\citep{schreiber2023surrogates,landman2023rff}. Whether this also holds for
hybrid diffusion models has, to our knowledge, not been tested with controls that
isolate the circuit.

We study it in the distillation setting described above. Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A hybrid latent consistency-distillation pipeline in which both the
  teacher and the student can contain a variational branch, with accounting of
  circuit evaluations, timing and finite-shot cost (Section~\ref{sec:method}).
  \item A set of controls that change one component at a time: an exact classical
  simulation of the same circuit without entangling gates, a size-matched
  classical branch, fully classical networks, and ablations that remove either
  the whole branch or only its input-dependent part (Section~\ref{sec:controls}).
  \item Preregistered sweeps over backbone width, qubit count, depth and data
  re-uploading, analysed with the preregistered per-cell criteria and, post hoc,
  with pooled equivalence intervals and a power analysis of the criteria
  (Sections~\ref{sec:res-sweeps} and~\ref{sec:res-equivalence}).
  \item Evidence that distillation transfers across four datasets and, at equal
  shot budget, beats running the teacher with fewer DDIM steps
  (Sections~\ref{sec:res-distill} and~\ref{sec:res-shots}).
\end{enumerate}

We make no claim of quantum advantage, and our circuits (4--8 qubits) are
trivially simulable. The question is narrower and, we think, prior to any
advantage claim: whether, in this model family, the circuit changes the
function the model learns by an amount we can detect.

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Diffusion, distillation and consistency models.}
DDIM turns a trained diffusion model into a deterministic sampler that can skip
steps \citep{song2021ddim}; DPM-Solver and its successors reach good quality in
10--20 evaluations with higher-order solvers \citep{lu2022dpmsolver}. Knowledge
distillation into a one-step generator \citep{luhman2021knowledge} and
progressive distillation \citep{salimans2022progressive}, which also introduced
the $v$-parameterisation we use for the teacher, train students to imitate the
teacher's sampler. Consistency distillation trains a student to give the same
output at adjacent points of the teacher's trajectory, with an
exponential-moving-average (EMA) target and a boundary condition at $t=0$
\citep{song2023consistency}; improved techniques refine the training of
consistency models \citep{song2024improved}, and latent consistency models apply
the idea in the latent space of a pretrained latent diffusion model
\citep{luo2023lcm}. We follow the original consistency-distillation recipe in a
small latent space with a cosine noise schedule \citep{nichol2021improved}.

\paragraph{Quantum and hybrid diffusion models.}
QuDDPM and its mixed-state extension define diffusion over quantum states
\citep{zhang2023quddpm,kwun2024mixedquddpm}. For classical images,
\citet{kolle2024qddm} propose quantum denoising diffusion models and a unitary
single-sampling variant inspired by consistency models;
\citet{zhang2025oqiddm} combine optimized quantum implicit neural networks with
consistency models for $O(1)$-step sampling. We could only access the abstract
and a partial preview of this work, so we cannot compare against its training
objective. In latent space, \citet{defalco2025qldm} use an autoencoder with a
10-dimensional latent and variational circuits for image, time and their
combination, \citet{yeteraydeniz2025medical} use quantum residual blocks for
medical images, and \citet{qian2026hybrid} run a mixed-state quantum diffusion
model in an autoencoder's latent space. To our knowledge, none of the works whose
full text we could read distils a hybrid teacher into a few-step student, and
none includes a control that keeps the architecture fixed and removes only the
circuit or only its entanglement.

\paragraph{Benchmarking and dequantisation.}
\citet{bowles2024better} benchmark twelve quantum classifiers against simple
classical models and find that the classical models usually win and that
removing entanglement often does not hurt. \citet{schreiber2023surrogates} show
that large classes of re-uploading models have efficiently computable classical
surrogates, and random-Fourier-feature models approximate many variational
models classically \citep{landman2023rff}. More broadly, the role of advantage as
the goal of quantum machine learning \citep{schuld2022advantage}, classical
shadows of quantum models \citep{jerbi2024shadows}, and the link between
trainability and classical simulability \citep{cerezo2023simulability} have been
examined. The function computed by a circuit with angle encoding is a truncated
Fourier series in the inputs whose frequencies are set by the encoding, and
repeating the encoding (data re-uploading) enlarges the frequency spectrum
\citep{schuld2021encoding}; re-uploading also makes even single-qubit
classifiers universal \citep{perezsalinas2020reuploading}. Our separable
surrogate is an exact classical surrogate: without entangling gates, the circuit
factorises into independent single-qubit rotations of Bloch vectors.

\subsection{Latent space and noise schedule}

A convolutional autoencoder (two stride-2 convolutions, a linear layer to a
16-dimensional latent, and the mirror decoder) is trained for 20 epochs with a
pixel MSE loss on 10{,}000 training images (4{,}708 for PneumoniaMNIST, its full
training split). Latents are standardised with the per-dimension training mean
and standard deviation. We use $T=32$ discrete noise levels with a cosine
schedule \citep{nichol2021improved},
\begin{equation}
  \abar_t = \max\!\left(\cos^2\!\frac{\pi t}{2T},\,10^{-6}\right),\quad \abar_0=1,\qquad
  z_t=\sqrt{\abar_t}\,z_0+\sqrt{1-\abar_t}\,\epsilon,\ \ \epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I),
\end{equation}
so that $z_T$ is essentially pure noise and sampling starts from $\mathcal{N}(0,I)$.

\subsection{Network and branch}
\label{sec:network}

Every teacher and student uses the network of Figure~\ref{fig:arch}. With $u=t/T$,
the input features are $\phi=[z_t,\,u,\,\sin\pi u,\,\cos\pi u]$. The output
is the sum of a classical backbone (a three-layer MLP with SiLU activations
and hidden width $h$; $h=128$ unless stated) and a branch
\begin{equation}
  B(\phi)=W_r\,g\!\left(\pi\tanh(W_c\phi+b_c)\right)+b_r,
\end{equation}
where the compressor produces $2q$ angles $(\theta,\varphi)$ for $q$ qubits,
$g:\mathbb{R}^{2q}\to[-1,1]^q$ is the branch block, and $(W_r,b_r)$ is a linear
readout to the 16-dimensional latent. With $h=128$ and $q=4$, the branch has 256
of the network's 21{,}392 parameters (1.2\%).

\paragraph{Circuit.} The hybrid branch block prepares the product state
$\bigotimes_j R_Z(\varphi_j)R_Y(\theta_j)|0\rangle$, applies $L$ trainable layers
$U_\ell=\mathrm{CZ}_{\mathrm{ring}}\bigotimes_j R_Z(w_{\ell j2})R_Y(w_{\ell j1})$,
where $\mathrm{CZ}_{\mathrm{ring}}$ applies CZ between neighbouring qubits on a
ring, then a fixed $\bigotimes_j R_Y(\pi/4)$, and returns the exact expectation
values $\langle Z_j\rangle$. The final rotation makes phases and entanglement
visible to a $Z$ readout; without it, the last layer's $R_Z$ and CZ gates would
not affect the output. The circuit has $2qL$ trainable parameters (16 for
$q=4$, $L=2$), initialised from $\mathcal{N}(0,0.1^2)$, i.e.\ near the identity.
With data re-uploading, the encoding rotations are repeated before every
trainable layer. All circuits are simulated exactly as state vectors; the
data-independent gates of each forward pass are fused into one matrix, which is
tested against gate-by-gate simulation.

\subsection{Teacher}

The teacher predicts $v=\sqrt{\abar_t}\,\epsilon-\sqrt{1-\abar_t}\,z_0$
\citep{salimans2022progressive} and is trained with an MSE loss for 150 epochs
(AdamW \citep{loshchilov2019adamw} with PyTorch defaults, i.e.\ weight decay 0.01,
learning rate $10^{-3}$ without schedule, batch 64). It samples with deterministic
DDIM \citep{song2021ddim} on $k$ uniformly spaced levels between $T$ and $1$,
followed by a final step to $t=0$.

\subsection{Consistency distillation}

The student wraps the network in a parameterisation that enforces the
boundary condition $f_\theta(z,0)=z$:
\begin{equation}
  f_\theta(z_t,t)=\abar_t\,z_t+\sqrt{1-\abar_t}\,F_\theta(z_t,t).
  \label{eq:student}
\end{equation}
For a clean latent $z_0$, a level $t\sim\mathcal{U}\{1,\dots,T\}$ and noise
$\epsilon$, let $\hat z_{t-1}=\Phi(z_t;t\to t-1)$ be one DDIM step of the frozen
teacher. The loss is
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}\left\|f_\theta(z_t,t)-f_{\theta^-}(\hat z_{t-1},t-1)\right\|_2^2,
\end{equation}
where $\theta^-$ is an EMA of $\theta$ with decay $\mu$, updated after every
optimiser step \citep{song2023consistency}; the optimiser is as for the teacher.
Unless stated otherwise, students train for 200 epochs with $\mu=0.95$, a budget
chosen after the pilot (Section~\ref{sec:res-budget}). Students of different
kinds trained from the same teacher share the initialisation seed and the exact
sequence of minibatches and noise draws. All student evaluations use the EMA
weights $\theta^-$. One-step sampling returns $f_{\theta^-}(z_T,T)$. With $k$
steps, the student's prediction at each level is re-noised to the next level with
fresh noise before the next evaluation.

\subsection{Controls}
\label{sec:controls}

Each control changes one thing about the network of Section~\ref{sec:network}
and leaves everything else (data, autoencoder, teacher, seeds, minibatch order)
fixed.
\begin{description}
  \item[No entanglement.] The same circuit without CZ gates.
  \item[Separable surrogate.] An exact classical implementation of the
  no-entanglement circuit: each qubit is a Bloch vector rotated by the same
  $R_Y$ and $R_Z$ angles, and the output is $(b_z-b_x)/\sqrt2$, which equals
  $\langle Z\rangle$ after the final $R_Y(\pi/4)$. It computes the same function,
  with the same parameters and gradients, as the no-entanglement circuit, at cost
  linear in $q$. Unit tests confirm that it matches the no-entanglement circuit
  and differs from the entangled one on random inputs.
  \item[Classical branch.] The branch block is replaced by
  $\tanh(\mathrm{Linear}(2q\to q))$: same input, same output dimension and range,
  with cross-feature interactions (36 parameters for $q=4$, against the
  circuit's 16, which favours the control).
  \item[Classical.] No branch at all; only the backbone.
  \item[Branch ablations at evaluation.] In a trained model, either zero the
  readout (\texttt{readout\_zeroed}: removes the branch, including its learned
  bias), or replace the block's output $g$ by its mean over 2{,}048 noisy training
  latents at all noise levels (\texttt{features\_mean}). The latter removes the
  branch's dependence on both $z_t$ and $t$ but keeps its average output.
\end{description}

\subsection{Cost accounting}

Each sampling step evaluates the network once per sample, so a $k$-step
sampler with a circuit makes $k$ circuit evaluations per sample. We report
end-to-end wall-clock time for 512 samples, including the decoder, as the median
of five synchronised repetitions after two warm-up runs on one RTX~3080~Ti.
Simulated circuit time is not a proxy for hardware latency; for hardware cost
we report total measurement shots per sample (steps $\times$ shots per
evaluation) in Section~\ref{sec:res-shots}.

\section{Experimental Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

\paragraph{Datasets.} MNIST \citep{lecun1998mnist}, Fashion-MNIST
\citep{xiao2017fashion}, KMNIST \citep{clanuwat2018kmnist} and PneumoniaMNIST
from MedMNIST v2 \citep{yang2023medmnist} (two unbalanced classes,
1{,}214/3{,}494 training images; CC BY 4.0), all $28\times28$ greyscale.

\paragraph{Metric.} Following \citet{heusel2017fid}, we compute the Fréchet
distance between Gaussian fits to features of 512 generated samples and of real
test images (the first 1{,}000; all 624 for PneumoniaMNIST). The features are the
64-dimensional penultimate layer of a small CNN classifier trained separately for
each dataset, not Inception. We call this score \fidlike{}; lower is better. It is
only comparable within a dataset in this paper, never across datasets or to
published FID values, and with 512 samples it carries a finite-sample bias
\citep{chong2020effectively} that is shared by all models compared at the same
sample size. The classifiers were trained with budgets fixed before inspecting
the test split and reach held-out accuracies of 0.959 (MNIST), 0.889
(Fashion-MNIST), 0.931 (KMNIST) and 0.827 (PneumoniaMNIST). We also record the
entropy of the classifier's predicted labels as a diversity check
(Appendix~\ref{app:extra}, Table~\ref{tab:entropy}).

\paragraph{Teacher gate.} Before training students, each 32-step teacher had
to reach a label entropy of at least $0.8\ln 10$ (for PneumoniaMNIST, $0.8$
times the entropy of the training labels) and a mean classifier confidence of at
least 0.8 times that on real images. All 36 teachers of the dataset campaign
passed.

\paragraph{Seeds and runs.} Every configuration uses seeds 0, 1, 2; the seed
fixes the autoencoder, teacher and student, so each seed is an independent
replicate of the whole pipeline. Paired comparisons match seeds.

\paragraph{Stages, and what was fixed in advance.} The work proceeded in
three stages.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Pilot} (MNIST, hybrid teacher; students with 50 epochs,
  $\mu=0.999$), run under a written protocol with a proposed advance criterion.
  No student met it. We then ran a student-budget diagnostic outside that
  protocol, reusing the pilot's autoencoders and teachers and varying only epochs
  and $\mu$, and selected the student budget on the test-set \fidlike{}
  (Section~\ref{sec:res-budget}).
  \item \emph{Dataset and teacher campaign}: 4 datasets $\times$ 3 seeds; on each
  autoencoder, a hybrid, a classical and a no-entanglement teacher. The hybrid
  teacher is distilled into hybrid, classical and surrogate students; the
  control teachers into a classical student. This stage had no written
  preregistration. For MNIST, the autoencoders, hybrid teachers and their students
  are the runs from the budget diagnostic, copied rather than retrained, so MNIST
  student results are selected on the same metric and seeds
  (Sections~\ref{sec:res-distill}--\ref{sec:res-campaign-eq}).
  \item \emph{Preregistered extension} \textbf{E0--E4}, written into the
  repository's protocol before any of these runs
  (Sections~\ref{sec:res-e0}--\ref{sec:res-e4}). Preregistered were: E0, the
  ratio of branch to backbone RMS and the \fidlike{} with the readout zeroed
  (descriptive); E1, a capacity sweep over $h$; E2, circuit scaling with no
  backbone; E3, finite shots and noise at inference (descriptive); E4,
  diagnostics of multi-step degradation and of KMNIST (descriptive).
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Preregistered criteria (E1, E2).} In each cell (dataset and $h$, or
circuit configuration), with teacher and student of the same kind and size and
the one-step student's \fidlike{} as the primary metric: the \emph{circuit
contributes} if the hybrid model beats the classical branch in all three seeds
and its mean is at least 10\% lower; \emph{entanglement contributes} if the
hybrid beats the separable surrogate under the same rule. The 32-step teacher is
the preregistered secondary metric. Because E1 MNIST $h=0$ and E2 with 4 qubits,
depth 2 and no re-uploading are the same configuration, there are 23 unique cells.

\paragraph{Added after the preregistration.} The following are not part of the
preregistration and should be read as exploratory: the \texttt{features\_mean}
ablation and the RMS of the branch's input-varying part in E0; the four-step
student as an additional secondary metric in E1 and E2; all equivalence tests,
pooled intervals and the power analysis described next; and the analyses of the
campaign (stage 2).

\paragraph{Equivalence analysis (post hoc).} The preregistered criteria can
detect a contribution but cannot establish its absence. We therefore add two
one-sided tests (TOST) on paired log-ratios of \fidlike{} \citep{lakens2017tost}:
for each pair of models with the same seed (and dataset), $r=\log(\text{\fidlike{}}_A/\text{\fidlike{}}_B)$.
We report the geometric-mean ratio with a 90\% $t$-interval and call two models
\emph{equivalent} if the interval lies inside $[1/1.1,\,1.1]$, the same 10\% used
by the preregistered criteria. Intervals are computed at two levels: over all
paired runs as independent replicates, and with the cell (or dataset) as the
unit, i.e.\ a $t$-interval over per-cell mean log-ratios, which accounts for
differences between cells. Tables of \fidlike{} report arithmetic means; ratios
in the text and in equivalence tables are geometric means of paired ratios unless
stated.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Student budget: the pilot students were undertrained}
\label{sec:res-budget}

In the pilot, no student met the advance criterion we had proposed (in every
seed, \fidlike{} at most 10\% worse than the 32-step teacher, label entropy loss
at most $0.1\ln10$, and at least $2\times$ faster): the best one-step students
scored 347.5 (hybrid) and 426.9 (classical), against 24.7 for the 32-step
teacher (Table~\ref{tab:budget}). In this undertrained regime hybrid and classical
students also differed substantially (e.g.\ 137.1 against 106.9 at four steps).
Changing only the student's epochs and EMA decay resolved the failure. Lowering
$\mu$ to 0.95, or training four times longer, brings the one-step \fidlike{} to
28--32; we hypothesise, without having tested it, that with $\mu=0.999$ and 50
epochs the slowly moving target does not propagate the boundary condition up to
$t=T$. With 200 epochs and $\mu=0.95$, four-step hybrid, classical and surrogate
students meet the advance criterion in all three seeds, and the three kinds are
indistinguishable. We use this budget for all later students. Because it was
selected on the test metric, MNIST distillation results in the campaign are
optimistic; the other datasets and the E1--E3 sweeps reuse the budget without
further tuning.

\subsection{E1 and E2: no regime in which the circuit contributes detectably}
\label{sec:res-sweeps}

The preregistered sweeps are the most direct test of the circuit, because they
include regimes in which the branch carries most or all of the model. E1 shrinks
the backbone down to removing it ($h=0$; Figure~\ref{fig:capacity},
Table~\ref{tab:e1}); E2 scales the circuit with no backbone: 4, 6 and 8 qubits,
depth 2 and 4, with and without data re-uploading (Table~\ref{tab:e2}).

\paragraph{Preregistered criteria.} On the primary metric, the circuit criterion
is met in 1 of 23 unique cells and the entanglement criterion in none. The single
pass is MNIST with $h=16$ (hybrid 11\% better than the classical branch). There,
the separable surrogate is close to the hybrid (per seed 35.2/30.4/46.2 against
35.2/30.5/44.0), the backbone-only MLP is within seed noise, and the sign reverses
on Fashion-MNIST; we read it as an isolated pass of the separable function, which
is classically simulable. On the preregistered secondary metric (32-step teacher)
the circuit criterion passes in four cells: MNIST $h=128$, Fashion-MNIST $h=8$,
and E2 with 4 qubits and depth 4, with and without re-uploading. In the last
case without re-uploading the surrogate equals the hybrid (49.4 against 49.4);
with re-uploading it does not (52.1 against 49.1). The entanglement criterion
never passes on the teacher. On the non-preregistered four-step student, there
are two further circuit passes (MNIST $h=16$, Fashion-MNIST $h=32$) and one
entanglement pass (MNIST $h=4$, 14.5\% better than the surrogate). These
secondary passes are scattered, with no pattern in $h$, dataset or qubit count,
and are uncorrected for multiple comparisons.

\paragraph{Power of the criteria.} The criteria detect only large effects. From
the paired seed-to-seed spread of the log-ratios (median SD 0.10, interquartile
range 0.05--0.20 across cells), a simulation of the rule gives a per-cell
probability of passing of 0.43 for a true 10\% improvement, 0.76 for 15\% and
0.95 for 20\% at the median spread (0.31, 0.47, 0.64 at SD 0.20), and a
false-pass rate of 3--9\% when there is no effect (Appendix~\ref{app:extra},
Table~\ref{tab:power}). One pass in 23 cells is therefore consistent with no
effect, and single cells cannot exclude improvements of 10--15\%.

\paragraph{Pooled intervals.} Pooling cells, with the cell as the unit, gives much
tighter bounds (Table~\ref{tab:sweeppooled}). Over the 12 cells of E1, hybrid and
separable-surrogate students differ by $-0.0\%$ (90\% CI $[-2.9\%,+2.9\%]$),
equivalent within $\pm10\%$, and so do the six E2 configurations without
re-uploading ($+2.0\%$, $[-2.0\%,+6.1\%]$). With re-uploading, entanglement makes
the student worse: $+16.9\%$ ($[+0.2\%,+36.4\%]$). Against the classical branch,
the hybrid is $-4.0\%$ ($[-9.3\%,+1.5\%]$) at $h\ge16$, not significantly different,
but without equivalence; at $h\le8$ it is $+14.1\%$ ($[-1.7\%,+32.4\%]$), i.e.\
tending to be worse.

\paragraph{When the circuit carries the model, it tends to hurt.} With no or a
tiny backbone on Fashion-MNIST, the classical branch is much better than the
circuit (ratio of means 1.54 at $h=0$ and 1.31 at $h=4$). In E2, data re-uploading
makes every one-step hybrid student worse than the classical branch (ratios of
means 1.43--2.86, pooled $+111\%$) and much more variable across seeds, with no
consistent effect on the teacher. Without re-uploading, more qubits help
(one-step \fidlike{} 144, 86, 52 for 4, 6, 8 qubits at depth 2), but a classical
branch of the same width improves similarly (139, 73, 55). At $h=0$ the output is
an affine function of the $q$ branch outputs, so its rank is at most $q$; the gain
with $q$ is consistent with this readout rank and with the width of the
compressor ($2q$ angles), which the classical branch shares.

\paragraph{Parameter frontier.} Table~\ref{tab:e1} compares rows with the same $h$,
which do not have the same size. The preregistered frontier compares each hybrid
network with the best backbone-only MLP that has at most as many parameters
(Figure~\ref{fig:capacity} and Appendix~\ref{app:extra}, Table~\ref{tab:frontier}).
At the two smallest hybrid sizes (256 and 436 parameters), the MLP is better in
three of four cases (e.g.\ Fashion-MNIST: 124.2 with 180 parameters against 152.1
with 256). From 632 parameters upward, the hybrid is better or within seed noise;
the largest gap, Fashion-MNIST at $h=8$ (70.7 against 86.0 for an MLP with 376
parameters), is matched by the classical branch (67.9). A parameter-matched
comparison therefore does not favour the hybrid model in this sweep either.

\paragraph{Distillation needs a backbone.} With $h=0$, distillation largely fails:
the one-step students score 131--157 (99 for the Fashion-MNIST classical branch)
against 52--57 for the 32-step teachers. We attribute this, without a direct test,
to insufficient capacity for the one-step map. With 8 qubits and no re-uploading
the gap narrows (52.1 against 46.3), and the classical branch does the same (55.3).

\paragraph{Per-cell intervals.} Figure~\ref{fig:forest} shows the hybrid/control
ratio with 90\% intervals for all 46 primary comparisons. With three seeds, only 9
of the 46 are individually equivalent within $\pm10\%$, so the sweep does not
establish equivalence cell by cell; the pooled intervals above are the
equivalence evidence.

\subsection{Distillation transfers across datasets}
\label{sec:res-distill}

Figure~\ref{fig:steps} and Table~\ref{tab:campaign} show the dataset campaign
($h=128$, 4 qubits). Few-step students are far ahead of the teacher run with one to
four DDIM steps on all four datasets. How close the one-step student comes to the
full 32-step teacher depends on the dataset (Table~\ref{tab:studteach}): it is
within 10\% in every seed on Fashion-MNIST and KMNIST, better than the teacher on
PneumoniaMNIST, and 13\% worse on MNIST (within 10\% in one of three seeds), where
four steps are needed (8$\times$ fewer evaluations than the teacher). The
differences between datasets are large, so we do not report a single pooled
figure (with the dataset as the unit, the 90\% interval is
$[-16.5\%,+15.8\%]$). On MNIST, the one-step student reaches 28.0 in about 11~ms
for 512 samples, and the four-step student 24.3, against 24.7 in 328~ms for the
32-step teacher and 52.3 for the teacher with four steps. The advance criterion is
met in all seeds by the students of the hybrid teacher at 4 steps on MNIST, 1--8
steps on Fashion-MNIST and KMNIST, and 1--2 steps on PneumoniaMNIST. We compare
only against DDIM; higher-order solvers \citep{lu2022dpmsolver} would narrow the
gap at four to eight evaluations.

Students do not improve monotonically with steps. On MNIST and especially
PneumoniaMNIST, quality degrades beyond 4--8 steps (\fidlike{} $\approx100$ at 32
steps on PneumoniaMNIST, with large seed variance); on KMNIST it improves.
Section~\ref{sec:res-e4} traces this to a fraction of diverging samples.
KMNIST has high absolute \fidlike{} for every model; Section~\ref{sec:res-e4}
attributes this to the autoencoder.

\subsection{Campaign: hybrid and classical models are equivalent at $h=128$}
\label{sec:res-campaign-eq}
\label{sec:res-equivalence}

Teachers with a circuit, with a circuit without entanglement, and without any
branch have indistinguishable DDIM curves on all datasets
(Table~\ref{tab:campaign}). The same holds for the students of the hybrid
teacher (Table~\ref{tab:students}). Table~\ref{tab:equivalence} turns this into
equivalence tests. Every comparison is equivalent within $\pm10\%$ both with runs
and with datasets as the unit, and the dataset-level intervals lie within
$\pm4\%$; the conclusions would hold for any margin of at least 4\%. Excluding
KMNIST, whose generation quality is limited by its autoencoder
(Section~\ref{sec:res-e4}), changes the intervals by less than one percentage
point. The one interval that excludes zero favours the hybrid student over the
classical student by 1.5\%; that classical student has no branch at all, so this
comparison also changes the parameter count, whereas the hybrid student and its
separable surrogate differ by 0.2\%.

This regime is, however, the one in which equivalence is least informative about
the circuit: the branch is 1.2\% of the parameters, and E0 shows that its
input-dependent output is small. We include it because it is the configuration
used for distillation, not as the main evidence about the circuit, which comes
from Section~\ref{sec:res-sweeps}. We did not run a positive control showing that
the pipeline would detect a 10\% change caused by the branch.

Per sampling step, models with a simulated circuit are about 8 times slower than
classical ones at one step (10.8 against 1.3~ms) and about 13 times slower for the
32-step teacher (329 against 26~ms). Simulating the circuit without entanglement
costs the same (329~ms for the 32-step no-entanglement teacher), whereas the
separable surrogate computes that function about 5--6 times faster (2.2 against
10.8~ms at one step, 54 against 329~ms at 32 steps).

\subsection{E0: the branch acts mostly as a learned offset}
\label{sec:res-e0}

The branch is not ignored by the optimiser: zeroing its readout raises the
teacher's \fidlike{} by 19--100\% depending on the dataset, and the PneumoniaMNIST
student's from 5.8 to 22.2 (Table~\ref{tab:e0}). Most of this effect comes from
the branch's average output: the part that varies across samples at a fixed noise
level has an RMS of 5--11\% of the backbone's (median over $t$), and replacing the
block's output by its mean over samples and noise levels (\texttt{features\_mean})
changes \fidlike{} by $-0.1\%$ to $+6.9\%$ on average over seeds, with a single
seed reaching $+13.7\%$ (Table~\ref{tab:e0rel}). The surrogate and no-entanglement
models behave the same. This ablation was added after the preregistration. It also
removes the branch's dependence on $t$, so the cost of removing only the
$z_t$-dependence is at most this large. These small effects are consistent with
the insensitivity to shots and noise in Section~\ref{sec:res-shots}.

\subsection{E3: shots and noise}
\label{sec:res-shots}

We evaluated noiselessly trained MNIST models with finite shots and local
depolarising noise at inference. Each circuit evaluation is simulated as a
density matrix with depolarising probability $p$ on every qubit after the
encoding and after each trainable layer; all qubits are measured in the
computational basis with $S$ shots per evaluation, and $\langle Z_j\rangle$ are
estimated from the counts. Table~\ref{tab:e3} and Figure~\ref{fig:shots} show
$p\in\{0,0.03\}$ and $S\in\{\text{exact},64\}$; the full grid
($p\in\{0,0.001,0.01,0.03\}$, $S\in\{\text{exact},1024,256,64\}$) is in the
repository.

With the backbone ($h=128$), 64 shots and $p=0.03$ leave \fidlike{} unchanged, as E0
leads one to expect. At an equal total shot budget, the distilled student is better
than the teacher run with fewer DDIM steps: one step with 64 shots (64 shots per
sample) gives 27.9, against 52.2 for the four-step teacher (256 shots per sample);
the 32-step teacher needs 2{,}048 shots per sample to reach 24.7. Distillation
therefore reduces the quantum cost relative to shortening the DDIM schedule, but
for a circuit that, in this model, contributes little. Without the backbone,
distillation fails (Section~\ref{sec:res-sweeps}), and at budgets of 256 shots per
sample and above the teacher is better; we have no teacher configuration at 64 or
128 shots per sample to compare with. Without the backbone, noise and shots also
matter more: $p=0.03$ moves the one-step student from 144.4 to 158.4 (exact) and the
four-step student from 110.7 to 99.7, changes of about 10\% in both directions,
and 64-shot sampling slightly improves \fidlike{} (144.4 to 138.7), which suggests
that the metric can reward added variance.

\subsection{E4: why students degrade with steps, and the KMNIST bottleneck}
\label{sec:res-e4}

\paragraph{Multi-step degradation (E4a).} Measured against the endpoint of the
teacher's full DDIM trajectory from the same $z_t$, the student's error is small
at every $t$ (latent MSE 0.0003 at $t=1$ and 0.027 at $t=32$ on MNIST, against a
signal energy of about 1). When the input is the student's own re-noised output,
the error is higher at small $t$ (0.0005 at $t=1$, 0.012 against 0.009 at $t=8$) and
the same at $t=32$; these increases are small compared with the degradation in
\fidlike{}. Instead, a growing fraction of samples diverges when the student is
applied repeatedly (Table~\ref{tab:e4a}): the median latent energy stays near 1,
but the tail explodes, with maxima at 32 steps of $5\times10^2$--$4\times10^3$ on
MNIST and $10^5$--$7\times10^7$ on PneumoniaMNIST, consistent with the growing pixel
variance we observed. Clipping or rescaling the latent between steps, or training
on the student's own re-noised predictions, are possible fixes that we did not
test. Hybrid and surrogate students behave the same.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What the controls rule out.} The similarity of hybrid and classical
models could have several explanations: the optimiser could ignore the branch,
the circuit could compute something useful that a classical branch also computes,
or entanglement could matter but too little to detect. The controls separate
these. The branch is used (zeroing it hurts) but mostly through its average output
(E0). A classical layer of the same shape matches the circuit or beats it,
markedly so when the branch carries the model (E1, E2). Entanglement never
improved on the exact separable surrogate: pooled over E1 and over E2 without
re-uploading the two are equivalent within a few percent, and with re-uploading
entanglement made the student worse. In this model family, then, we find no
detectable contribution of the circuit, and certainly none from entanglement;
single cells cannot exclude effects of 10--15\%.

The absence of a benefit from entanglement is plausible from the structure of the
model. Without re-uploading, each of the $2q$ angles enters once, so each
$\langle Z_j\rangle$ is a low-degree trigonometric polynomial in those angles
\citep{schuld2021encoding}, followed by a learned linear readout, and the
compressor and readout trained jointly with the circuit can absorb much of what
the circuit's entangling layers would add. The readout uses only single-qubit
expectations after a shallow CZ ring, so entanglement can affect the output only
through local marginals; with weights initialised near the identity, the circuit
also starts close to a product of single-qubit rotations. Two-qubit correlator
readouts ($\langle Z_iZ_j\rangle$), or holding the readout rank fixed across $q$,
would be the natural next test of whether entanglement can matter at all in this
architecture. Re-uploading enlarges the frequency spectrum
\citep{schuld2021encoding} but, here, mainly made optimisation harder.

\paragraph{What remains.} Consistency distillation of a hybrid teacher reduces the
number of circuit evaluations by $8$--$32\times$ at a quality within 10\% of the
32-step teacher, depending on the dataset, and at an equal shot budget it is
better than running the teacher with fewer DDIM steps. We did not compare against
higher-order solvers or other distillation methods. This would matter for any
model in which the circuit could not be replaced; in the model family studied
here, it can be.

\paragraph{Implications for benchmarking.} Hybrid generative models are often
compared with classical models that have a similar number of parameters. In our
sweep, comparisons at equal backbone width ($h$) favoured the hybrid model at small
$h$; at equal parameter count they did not, and a classical branch of the same shape
showed that the circuit was not the reason for the hybrid's advantage over
narrower MLPs. We suggest that claims of a benefit from a circuit be accompanied by
(i) a classical block with the same inputs and outputs in the same place, (ii) an
exact separable surrogate when entanglement is claimed to matter, (iii) an ablation
that replaces the circuit's output by its mean, which separates a learned offset
from an input-dependent computation, and (iv) pooled intervals or a power
analysis, since three-seed per-cell comparisons cannot exclude moderate effects.

\paragraph{Limitations.} The results hold for one architecture: an additive
branch on a classical MLP in a 16-dimensional autoencoder latent, with 4--8
qubits, depth up to 4, angle encoding, a hardware-efficient ansatz and
single-qubit readout. Other placements, encodings, readouts or ans\"atze could
behave differently. Each cell has three seeds and 512 samples. The student budget
was selected on the test metric, MNIST campaign results reuse those runs, the
campaign was not preregistered, and the equivalence analysis, its margin and
several ablations were added after the preregistered runs (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}).
There is no correction for multiple comparisons in the per-cell criteria and no
positive control. \fidlike{} uses features of a small classifier
(82.7--95.9\% accuracy), 512 generated samples and, for PneumoniaMNIST, 624 real
images; it is not comparable to standard FID, we report no precision/recall
\citep{kynkaanniemi2019improved} or latent-space metrics, and real-data and
reconstruction floors were measured only for KMNIST. The low PneumoniaMNIST scores
(5--7) may be close to that dataset's floor. Noise was applied only at inference,
with a simple local depolarising model and no readout error or crosstalk; training
with shots or noise was not tested. Few-step baselines are limited to DDIM. We
could not read the full text of the closest consistency-based quantum diffusion
model \citep{zhang2025oqiddm}. Finally, our circuits are small enough to simulate
exactly, so nothing here bears on regimes where simulation is hard.

\paragraph{Reproducibility and history.} An earlier version of this project
(\texttt{v1/} in the repository) used a classical teacher and an earlier
training procedure. It contained implementation errors, documented in the
repository, and its raw outputs could not be fully recovered; none of its
numbers are used here. Every number in this paper is regenerated from the
committed JSON reports by \texttt{paper/analysis/make\_assets.py}; training and
evaluation code, configurations, the preregistration and environment records
are in \texttt{v2/}.

\section{Conclusion}

We distilled hybrid quantum latent diffusion teachers into few-step consistency
students and asked, with controls that change one component at a time, whether the
circuit contributes. Across 23 preregistered capacity and scaling cells, the
circuit met the contribution criterion against a size-matched classical branch
once, and entanglement never beat an exact separable surrogate; pooled over cells,
hybrid and surrogate are equivalent within about 3\%, and with data re-uploading
entanglement made results worse. In the trained models, the branch acts mostly as
a learned offset. Distillation transferred across four datasets: few-step students
came within 10\% of the 32-step teacher with 8--32$\times$ fewer circuit
evaluations and, at equal shot budget, beat the teacher run with fewer DDIM steps.
We see this as a negative result for one model family and as a case for including
such controls in hybrid generative benchmarks. Natural next steps are
architectures in which the circuit cannot be bypassed, such as models of quantum
data \citep{zhang2023quddpm}, where distilling a few-step student would reduce a
cost that cannot be avoided classically.
