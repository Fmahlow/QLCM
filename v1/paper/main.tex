\begin{abstract}
Diffusion models have become a leading approach to image synthesis, but their
iterative sampling procedures remain expensive. Consistency models and latent
consistency models reduce this cost by learning mappings that send noisy points
along a diffusion trajectory directly toward a common clean endpoint. In
parallel, quantum generative learning has explored parameterized quantum circuits
as compact function classes for near-term quantum machine learning, including
recent quantum denoising diffusion models. This paper proposes Quantum Latent
Consistency Models (QLCMs), a hybrid quantum-classical architecture that
distills a latent diffusion teacher into a parameterized quantum consistency
student. The student receives a noisy latent state and a noise level, encodes
them into a simulated variational quantum circuit, and predicts a clean latent
endpoint or a residual correction to a classical consistency head. We formulate
the QLCM training objective, describe one-step and few-step samplers, and propose
a controlled evaluation protocol on MNIST and Fashion-MNIST against
parameter-matched classical consistency students and latent diffusion baselines.
The central question is not whether the model demonstrates quantum advantage,
but whether quantum consistency modules provide useful inductive bias or
parameter efficiency in low-dimensional latent image generation. In simulator
experiments on MNIST and Fashion-MNIST, using genuine consistency
distillation from a converged latent diffusion teacher and 3 seeds per
configuration, the residual quantum placement (QLCM-R) does not outperform a
parameter-matched classical consistency student: it has 54\% more trainable
parameters (139{,}640 vs. 90{,}640) and a slightly worse classifier-feature
FID-like score ($359.4\pm3.0$ vs. $346.7\pm16.0$), with statistically
indistinguishable sample diversity and recognizability. Using the quantum
circuit as the primary consistency head (QLCM-H) trains to a substantially
higher loss and collapses sample diversity (label entropy 1.05--1.67 vs.\
$\approx$2.03 out of a maximum of 2.303), confirming a failure mode
anticipated at the design stage and replicated across two independent
training procedures. We also report a methodological finding: an earlier
version of this evaluation misattributed unstable few-step sampling to the
multistep sampler alone; fixing the sampler was necessary but not
sufficient, and few-step sampling only stabilized once the training script
was corrected to perform teacher distillation rather than training from data
alone. We report the quantum-placement result as a negative finding for the
architectures and dataset scale tested, together with the full evidence
package needed to reproduce or challenge it.
\end{abstract}

\textbf{Keywords:} quantum machine learning; consistency models; latent
diffusion; generative modeling; synthetic image generation; variational quantum
circuits

\section{Introduction}

Denoising diffusion probabilistic models (DDPMs) generate high-quality images by
learning to reverse a gradual noising process \citep{ho2020ddpm}. Their
performance has made diffusion-based methods a central class of modern
generative models, but sampling typically requires many neural function
evaluations. Latent diffusion models (LDMs) reduce the computational burden by
moving the denoising process from pixel space into the latent space of an
autoencoder \citep{rombach2022ldm}. Consistency models address a different part
of the same bottleneck: instead of learning only a local denoising step, they
learn a map that sends points along a probability-flow trajectory to a common
clean endpoint, allowing one-step or few-step generation
\citep{song2023consistency}. Latent Consistency Models (LCMs) combine this idea
with latent diffusion and demonstrate fast synthesis through consistency
distillation \citep{luo2023lcm}.

Quantum machine learning offers another route for compact generative modeling.
Parameterized quantum circuits can represent nonlinear feature maps and
trainable measurement functions, and quantum generative models have been
studied across adversarial, Born-machine, and diffusion-inspired formulations.
Recent quantum denoising diffusion work has brought diffusion-style training
into quantum and hybrid settings, including image-oriented quantum denoising
architectures, a quantum single-sampling consistency-inspired architecture, and
quantum-data diffusion models
\citep{kolle2024qddm,zhang2023quddpm,kwun2024mixedquddpm}. These results show
that the intersection of quantum circuits and diffusion models is already
active. They also make broad novelty claims inappropriate: a new contribution
must specify what changes in the learning objective, architecture, and empirical
question.

This paper proposes Quantum Latent Consistency Models (QLCMs). QLCMs transfer
the consistency-model objective into a hybrid quantum-classical latent image
generator. A classical encoder maps an image to a compact latent vector. A
classical latent diffusion teacher defines trajectories in this latent space. A
hybrid student then learns a consistency function in which a parameterized
quantum circuit contributes either the direct clean-latent prediction, a
time-conditioning signal, or a residual correction to a classical consistency
head. This design differs from prior quantum image diffusion work by combining
latent-space teacher-student consistency distillation with explicit ablations of
where a quantum circuit should enter the consistency map.

The intended contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item a formal QLCM architecture for simulator-based few-step image
    generation;
    \item three quantum consistency placements: direct head, residual
    correction, and time-conditioning;
    \item a distillation objective that adapts latent consistency training to a
    parameterized quantum student;
    \item a reproducible evaluation protocol on MNIST and Fashion-MNIST with
    parameter-matched classical baselines.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Diffusion, Latent Diffusion, and Consistency Models}

DDPMs formulate image generation as the learned reversal of a Markovian
diffusion process \citep{ho2020ddpm}. Although effective, the reverse process is
sequential and can require many denoising evaluations. LDMs address part of this
cost by training diffusion models in a perceptually meaningful latent space
rather than directly in pixel space \citep{rombach2022ldm}. Consistency models
further target sampling cost by learning a function whose outputs remain
consistent across points on the same probability-flow trajectory
\citep{song2023consistency}. LCMs extend this strategy to pretrained latent
diffusion models and enable few-step synthesis in latent space
\citep{luo2023lcm}. QLCM adopts this trajectory-consistency idea but changes the
student function class by introducing a quantum circuit into the consistency
map.

\subsection{Quantum Generative Models and Quantum Diffusion}

Quantum generative modeling studies how quantum circuits can parameterize
probability distributions, feature maps, and trainable measurement functions.
Recent quantum diffusion work is especially relevant. \citet{zhang2023quddpm}
introduced denoising diffusion probabilistic modeling for quantum data, while
\citet{kwun2024mixedquddpm} extended quantum diffusion to a mixed-state setting.
For classical image generation, \citet{kolle2024qddm} proposed quantum denoising
diffusion architectures evaluated on datasets including MNIST and
Fashion-MNIST, and also introduced a unitary single-sampling architecture
motivated by consistency modeling. This is the closest prior work. QLCM is
therefore positioned more narrowly: it studies latent-space consistency
distillation from a classical teacher into quantum-hybrid students and compares
three circuit placements under matched small-model constraints.

\subsection{Latent Representation}

Let $x_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W}$ denote an image. An encoder
$E_\phi$ maps the image to a latent vector $z_0 = E_\phi(x_0)$, and a decoder
$D_\psi$ reconstructs an image $\hat{x}_0 = D_\psi(z_0)$. In the first
experiments, $E_\phi$ and $D_\psi$ may be implemented as a small convolutional
autoencoder or as a deterministic low-dimensional transform for controlled
ablation. The latent dimension is chosen to be small enough for quantum-circuit
feature encoding under statevector simulation.

\subsection{Latent Diffusion Teacher}
\label{sec:teacher}

The teacher defines a forward noising process in latent space using a
discrete variance-preserving schedule with $T$ timesteps and per-step
variances $\beta_t$, $\alpha_t = 1-\beta_t$, $\bar\alpha_t=\prod_{k\le t}\alpha_k$:
\begin{equation}
    z_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, z_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon,\quad
    \epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I).
    \label{eq:forward-noise}
\end{equation}
A classical noise-prediction network $\epsilon_\varphi(z_t,t)$ is trained with
the standard denoising objective $\mathbb{E}\|\epsilon_\varphi(z_t,t)-\epsilon\|_2^2$
on real encoder latents. This teacher is frozen after training and used only
to construct trajectory pairs for consistency distillation
(Section~\ref{sec:consistency-objective}); it is not itself used at sampling
time.

\subsection{Quantum Consistency Student}
\label{sec:quantum-student}

The QLCM student is a function
\begin{equation}
    f_\theta(z_t, t) \rightarrow \hat{z}_0,
\end{equation}
where $\hat{z}_0$ estimates the clean latent endpoint associated with $z_t$.
The student contains a feature compressor, a parameterized quantum circuit, and
a classical readout. A typical circuit receives an encoded vector
$u(z_t,t)$ through angle rotations, applies $L$ layers of trainable rotations and
entangling gates, and returns expectation values
\begin{equation}
    q_\theta(z_t,t) =
    \left[
    \langle Z_1 \rangle,\ldots,\langle Z_n \rangle
    \right].
\end{equation}
These measurement features are decoded into a latent endpoint prediction.

We consider three variants:
\begin{description}
    \item[QLCM-H.] The quantum circuit acts as the main consistency head.
    \item[QLCM-R.] The quantum circuit predicts a residual correction to a
    classical consistency estimate.
    \item[QLCM-T.] The quantum circuit produces a time-conditioning vector that
    modulates a classical consistency network.
\end{description}

QLCM-R is expected to be the most stable variant because the classical path can
learn the dominant trajectory structure while the quantum circuit learns a
compact correction.

\subsection{Consistency Objective}
\label{sec:consistency-objective}

We use consistency \emph{distillation} (CD) rather than consistency training
from data alone \citep{song2023consistency}: a frozen classical latent
diffusion teacher (Section~\ref{sec:teacher}) is required to construct the
trajectory pair $(z_t, z_s)$, $s<t$, that the student is trained to agree on.
Given a clean latent $z_0$ and a sampled pair $s<t$, we first forward-noise
$z_0$ to $z_t$ as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:forward-noise}. We then take one deterministic DDIM step of the
\emph{teacher} from $z_t$ at level $t$ down to level $s$: the teacher predicts
$\hat\epsilon = \epsilon_\varphi(z_t,t)$, which gives a teacher-side clean
estimate $\hat{z}_0 = (z_t - \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\hat\epsilon)/\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}$,
re-noised to level $s$ along the same direction:
\begin{equation}
    z_s = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_s}\,\hat{z}_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_s}\,\hat\epsilon.
\end{equation}
This makes $z_s$ a point on the teacher's empirical probability-flow ODE
trajectory through $z_t$, not an independent noisy view of $z_0$; the
teacher's parameters $\varphi$ are frozen and receive no gradient. The
consistency loss then encourages the student's predictions at both trajectory
points to agree:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cons}} =
    \left\|
    f_\theta(z_t,t) -
    \mathrm{sg}\left(f_\theta(z_s,s)\right)
    \right\|_2^2,
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{sg}(\cdot)$ stops gradients. Our implementation stops gradients
on a second forward pass of the same online student rather than an
exponential-moving-average target network; we note this as a simplification
relative to \citet{song2023consistency} rather than claim EMA distillation.
Because clean latents are available in the encoder-decoder setting, we add an
endpoint reconstruction term:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{target}} =
    \left\|
    f_\theta(z_t,t) - z_0
    \right\|_2^2.
\end{equation}
The combined training objective is
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{QLCM}} =
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cons}} +
    \lambda \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{target}}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Few-Step Sampling}
\label{sec:few-step-sampling}

At inference time, sampling begins from $z_T \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I)$. In the
one-step setting, the student directly predicts $\hat{z}_0=f_\theta(z_T,T)$ and
the decoder produces $\hat{x}_0=D_\psi(\hat{z}_0)$. In the few-step setting, a
short sequence of noise levels is used, allowing optional re-noising or teacher
schedule corrections between student evaluations. The primary evaluation will
compare one-, two-, and four-step sampling.

\section{Experimental Protocol}

\subsection{Datasets}

The first benchmarks are MNIST and Fashion-MNIST. These datasets are small
enough for simulator-based quantum experiments while still exposing meaningful
differences in sample recognizability and class diversity. A synthetic
Bars-and-Stripes dataset is used only for smoke tests.

\subsection{Baselines}

The planned baselines are:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item a classical latent diffusion teacher;
    \item a parameter-matched classical latent consistency student;
    \item a compact classical MLP consistency student;
    \item existing quantum-denoising style baselines when reproducible settings
    are available.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Ablations}

The ablation grid varies the QLCM placement, number of qubits, circuit depth,
feature encoding, latent dimension, and number of sampling steps. All reported
models will use fixed seeds and matched preprocessing. Parameter counts and
wall-clock time will be reported alongside sample-quality metrics.

\subsection{Metrics}

The evaluation will report reconstruction quality for the latent autoencoder,
sample grids, classifier-based recognizability, label entropy, KID or FID-like

low-resolution grayscale datasets, metric interpretation will be conservative:
visual grids and classifier-based diagnostics will be treated as complementary

\subsection{Reproducibility and Environment}

All reported runs use the public training/evaluation scripts in this
repository (\texttt{scripts/train\_autoencoder.py},
\texttt{scripts/train\_teacher.py}, \texttt{scripts/train\_qlcm.py},
\texttt{scripts/evaluate\_qlcm.py}), with configs
\texttt{configs/mnist.yaml} and \texttt{configs/fashion\_mnist.yaml}
unchanged except for setting \texttt{device: cuda}. Both the "quantum" circuit
and all classical components are simulated in PyTorch on a single NVIDIA RTX
2080 Ti GPU (PyTorch 2.8.0+cu128); there is no execution on quantum hardware
and no claim of quantum advantage. MNIST and Fashion-MNIST subsets of 10{,}000
training images are used, with a shared autoencoder (20 epochs) and latent
diffusion teacher (150 epochs, $T=50$) per dataset, distilled into consistency
students (50 epochs, consistency distillation as in
Section~\ref{sec:consistency-objective}) under each placement/qubit/depth
configuration. Random seeds are fixed via \texttt{torch.manual\_seed} per run;
this seeding was a gap in the original training scripts identified and fixed
during this experiment campaign (the seed field in config files was
previously read but never applied). The primary MNIST comparison (classical
vs.\ QLCM-R, 4 qubits, depth 2) and every placement/qubit/depth ablation cell
on MNIST are repeated at seeds 0, 1, 2; Fashion-MNIST rows also use 3 seeds
for the classical and QLCM-R (4 qubits, depth 2) configurations. All metrics
are produced by \texttt{scripts/evaluate\_qlcm.py} and aggregated without
modification by \texttt{scripts/aggregate\_results.py} into
\texttt{results/summary.csv}, which is the source of every number in this
section and in Tables~\ref{tab:ablation} and~\ref{tab:main-results}.

Three issues were found and fixed during this campaign, before any number
below was trusted: (1) \texttt{scripts/train\_qlcm.py} never loaded the
trained teacher and trained students purely from noised ground-truth latents,
which is consistency \emph{training} (CT), not the consistency
\emph{distillation} (CD) the method section describes; we implemented the
teacher-driven DDIM step in Section~\ref{sec:consistency-objective} so the
code now matches the claim. (2) the multistep sampler re-noised with an
arbitrary small perturbation instead of the schedule's actual forward
process; fixed to reuse \texttt{schedule.add\_noise}, matching Song et al.'s
Algorithm 1. (3) the latent diffusion teacher was undertrained (50 epochs,
noise-prediction MSE still falling at $\approx$0.88); increased to 150
epochs, after which MNIST and Fashion-MNIST teachers plateau at
$\approx$0.85 and $\approx$0.76 respectively. All results below reflect all
three fixes; the single-seed, pre-fix numbers from an earlier version of this
manuscript are archived in \texttt{results/\_archive\_v1} and
\texttt{results/\_archive\_v2\_ct\_fixed\_sampler} for comparison and are
superseded by this section.

The FID-like metric uses 64-dimensional penultimate-layer features from a
small CNN classifier (2 conv layers, 1 hidden layer) trained for 3 epochs on
the real training split of the corresponding dataset, not an Inception
network; it is only meaningful for relative comparison within this paper and
is not comparable to FID values reported elsewhere in the literature.
Recognizability is the same classifier's mean softmax confidence on generated
samples. Label entropy is the Shannon entropy (in nats) of the classifier's
predicted-label histogram over 512 generated samples, with a maximum of
$\ln 10 \approx 2.303$ for a perfectly uniform class distribution; this is a
generation-side diversity diagnostic and not a measure of sample fidelity to
any specific ground-truth label.

\section{Results and Analysis}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Primary MNIST Comparison: QLCM-R vs.\ Classical}

Table~\ref{tab:main-results} reports the primary comparison. Over three seeds,
the classical one-step consistency student reaches a classifier-feature
FID-like score of $346.7 \pm 16.0$ with $90{,}640$ parameters. The residual
quantum student (QLCM-R, 4 qubits, depth 2) reaches $359.4 \pm 3.0$ with
$139{,}640$ parameters --- 54\% more parameters for a slightly \emph{worse}
FID-like score, and statistically indistinguishable recognizability
($0.617\pm0.008$ vs.\ $0.609\pm0.010$) and label entropy ($2.032\pm0.017$ vs.\
$2.037\pm0.021$, out of a maximum of 2.303). The training-loss trajectories of
the two students are nearly identical (final \texttt{combined\_qlcm\_loss} of
0.106 for classical vs.\ 0.106 for QLCM-R, averaged over seeds), and at
matched random latent draws the two students produce visually similar sample
grids (Figures~\ref{fig:mnist-classical} and~\ref{fig:mnist-qlcmr}). We do not
find evidence that the residual quantum module improves generation quality,
diversity, or parameter efficiency over the classical baseline at this scale;
if anything, it adds parameters without a measurable benefit. This matches
the ``weaker but informative'' outcome anticipated in the experimental
protocol, not the strongest hypothesized outcome, and is consistent across
two independent training schemes: an earlier, uncorrected consistency
\emph{training} run without teacher distillation showed the same qualitative
gap between QLCM-R and the classical baseline (archived in
\texttt{results/\_archive\_v2\_ct\_fixed\_sampler}).

\subsection{Placement Ablation: Head, Residual, and Time-Conditioning}

Table~\ref{tab:ablation} compares quantum placements on MNIST, now with 3
seeds per cell. The direct quantum head (QLCM-H) remains the clearest
negative result under proper consistency distillation: training loss
plateaus around 1.59--1.61 (4 qubits, depths 1--2) or 1.05 (6 qubits, depth
2), roughly an order of magnitude above the $\approx$0.106 reached by
classical, residual, and time-conditioning placements. QLCM-H's
classifier-feature FID-like scores (330.2--377.5) are numerically competitive
with or better than the other placements (359.4--389.3), but this is driven
by mode collapse rather than sample quality: QLCM-H's label entropy
(1.05--1.67, with seed-to-seed standard deviations up to 0.23) is well below
classical/residual/time placements (2.01--2.05, standard deviations
$\le$0.03), meaning the FID-like metric is computed over a much narrower,
less diverse, and less stable set of predicted digit classes across seeds.
This is consistent with the synthetic Bars-and-Stripes smoke test, where
QLCM-H also converged far more slowly (loss 2.17 after 5 epochs) than
classical or residual placements (loss 0.09). We treat this as confirming
evidence, replicated across two independent training schemes (CT and CD) and
3 seeds per cell, for the design-time hypothesis that a quantum circuit is
not yet competitive as the primary consistency bottleneck, at least at 4--6
qubits and depth 1--2 under statevector simulation. Residual (QLCM-R) and
time-conditioning (QLCM-T) placements both train to loss parity with the
classical baseline and do not show the QLCM-H diversity collapse or its
large seed-to-seed variance.

Increasing qubits from 4 to 6 at fixed depth 2 changes the residual
placement's 3-seed mean FID-like score from 369.7 (depth 1) to 359.4 (depth
2, 4 qubits) to 368.5 (6 qubits, depth 2), and the head placement's from
330.2 to 377.5 to 356.5 over the same progression. None of these differences
exceed roughly 1--2 standard deviations of their own seed spread
($\pm$3.0--21.9 FID-like points depending on cell), so we draw no
qubit-or-depth-scaling conclusion beyond noting that whatever effect exists
is not large or consistent enough at this qubit range (4--6) to separate from
seed variance.

\subsection{Few-Step Sampling Results}
\label{sec:few-step-results}

An earlier version of this evaluation found FID-like scores getting sharply
worse with more sampling steps and attributed it to the multistep sampler
injecting an arbitrary small perturbation instead of following the trained
noise schedule. We fixed the sampler to reuse \texttt{schedule.add\_noise}
(Section~\ref{sec:few-step-sampling}) and, independently, fixed consistency
training to be genuine teacher distillation (Section~\ref{sec:consistency-objective}).
With both fixes, few-step FID-like scores are essentially flat: classical
1/2/4-step scores of 337.8/350.3/342.5 and QLCM-R scores of
355.6/363.5/355.9 on MNIST (seed 0), and 424.0/421.2/415.7 (classical) and
419.5/415.7/407.3 (QLCM-R) on Fashion-MNIST (seed 0) --- if anything, 4-step
sampling is marginally \emph{better} than 1-step on Fashion-MNIST for both
placements. This is a substantially different outcome from the pre-fix
version of this experiment (archived in
\texttt{results/\_archive\_v2\_ct\_fixed\_sampler}, where FID-like still
degraded by 15--30\% from 1-step to 4-step after the sampler fix alone), which
indicates the earlier degradation was driven primarily by training the
student without ever consulting the teacher (consistency training, not
distillation) rather than by the sampler mechanics in isolation. We treat
this as a methodological lesson as much as a result: an isolated component
fix (the sampler) was insufficient, and only fixing the training procedure to
match what the paper claims resolved the few-step degradation.

\subsection{Fashion-MNIST}
\label{sec:fmnist-results}

The Fashion-MNIST classical and QLCM-R (4 qubits, depth 2) students follow
the same pattern as MNIST across 3 seeds: QLCM-R does not improve on the
classical baseline (FID-like $419.0\pm4.3$ vs.\ $419.7\pm3.1$, within seed
noise of each other; recognizability $0.513\pm0.006$ vs.\
$0.514\pm0.005$, also indistinguishable). Both students show pronounced mode
collapse toward a single garment class (337/512 and 301/512 samples
respectively predicted as class 0, ``T-shirt/top,'' on seed 0; label entropy
$1.333\pm0.066$ and $1.410\pm0.023$ out of a 2.303 maximum, both seeds 0--2),
visible in Figure~\ref{fig:fmnist-classical}. Unlike the few-step sampling
result, this collapse persisted after both the teacher-strength and
training-procedure fixes, so we no longer attribute it primarily to those
issues; it is more likely a consequence of the small 10{,}000-image training
subset and 50-epoch student budget, common to both placements and not
specific to the quantum module.

\subsection{Summary of Findings}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item QLCM-R does not match or exceed the classical consistency baseline
    on MNIST or Fashion-MNIST at the tested scale, across 3 seeds each: it
    uses 54\% more parameters for an equal or slightly worse FID-like score,
    with statistically indistinguishable recognizability and diversity.
    \item Using the quantum circuit as the direct consistency head (QLCM-H)
    is a clear, well-replicated failure mode: roughly $10\times$ higher
    training loss and pronounced, seed-unstable mode collapse, replicated
    independently on the synthetic smoke dataset, on MNIST across 3 seeds,
    and under two different training procedures (consistency training and
    consistency distillation).
    \item Residual and time-conditioning placements are stable and train to
    loss parity with the classical baseline, but provide no measured
    advantage over it, across the 4--6 qubit / depth 1--2 range tested.
    \item Two implementation issues were found and fixed during this
    campaign: the training script silently performed consistency
    \emph{training} instead of the consistency \emph{distillation} described
    in the method, and the multistep sampler renoised with an ad hoc
    perturbation instead of the trained schedule. Fixing only the sampler
    left few-step sampling degrading; fixing both resolved it, which we take
    as evidence the training procedure was the primary cause.
    \item Fashion-MNIST mode collapse toward a single class persisted after
    both fixes and after strengthening the teacher, and is likely attributable
    to the small training subset and student budget rather than to either
    the sampler or the quantum module.
    \item All MNIST placement/qubit/depth cells and the Fashion-MNIST
    classical/QLCM-R comparison now have 3-seed statistics; step-count
    scaling (2-step, 4-step) remains single-seed (seed 0) for all
    configurations.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Discussion}

The proposed QLCM architecture shifts the role of the quantum circuit from
iterative denoising to trajectory-consistent latent prediction. This change
matters because it imports a specific acceleration principle from modern
classical diffusion research into quantum generative modeling. It also creates a
clear empirical test: whether a compact quantum module can serve as a useful
student in consistency distillation.

The study has several limitations by design. First, all experiments are
simulator-based and therefore do not establish hardware quantum advantage.
Second, MNIST and Fashion-MNIST are low-complexity datasets; the negative
result reported here would not necessarily generalize to natural images,
larger latent spaces, or larger teachers. Third, statevector simulation
limits the number of qubits and circuit depth tested (4--6 qubits, depth
1--2); the literature-recommended 8-qubit configuration was not run in this
campaign and remains future work. Fourth, parameter matching does not fully
equalize optimization difficulty between classical and quantum students --
the quantum students have more parameters, not fewer, which strengthens
rather than weakens the negative finding for QLCM-R. Fifth, our consistency
distillation target network is the online student itself under
\texttt{no\_grad}, not an exponential moving average as in
\citet{song2023consistency}; an EMA target is a plausible way to further
stabilize training and was not tested here. Sixth, the Fashion-MNIST mode
collapse (Section~\ref{sec:fmnist-results}) was not resolved by any fix applied in this campaign
and limits how much weight the Fashion-MNIST numbers should carry relative to
MNIST.

The empirical results obtained here argue against the strongest hypothesized
outcome from the research plan (QLCM-R matching the classical baseline with
fewer parameters) and instead support the weaker, still-informative outcome:
under matched simulator conditions and across 3 seeds per configuration, a
residual or time-conditioning quantum module is trainable and stable but
offers no measured benefit, while using the quantum circuit as the primary
consistency bottleneck reliably fails to train and collapses sample
diversity. We see this as useful negative evidence for a specific,
falsifiable architectural claim, consistent with the framing adopted at the
research-design stage, rather than as a reason to abandon the broader
question. Unlike an earlier version of this evaluation, the few-step sampling
result is no longer inconclusive: after fixing both the sampler and, more
importantly, the training procedure to perform genuine teacher distillation
(Section~\ref{sec:few-step-results}), few-step sampling quality is stable rather than degrading. The
process of reaching that conclusion is itself a finding worth stating
plainly: a plausible-looking bug fix (the sampler) was not sufficient on its
own, and only tracing the degradation to its training-time root cause
resolved it. This is offered as a caution against fixing the most visible
symptom of a negative result without verifying it addresses the underlying
cause.

\section{Conclusion}

This paper proposes Quantum Latent Consistency Models as a hybrid
quantum-classical approach to few-step synthetic image generation. QLCM
distills a latent diffusion teacher into a quantum consistency student via
consistency distillation, aiming to combine the sampling efficiency of
consistency models with the compact trainable feature maps of parameterized
quantum circuits. We completed a reproducible MNIST and Fashion-MNIST
benchmark (Section~\ref{sec:results}) comparing classical, residual-quantum (QLCM-R),
head-quantum (QLCM-H), and time-conditioning-quantum (QLCM-T) consistency
students under matched training budgets, with 3 seeds per configuration for
every MNIST placement/qubit/depth cell and for the Fashion-MNIST
classical/QLCM-R comparison. At the tested scale (4--6 simulated qubits,
depth 1--2, 10{,}000-image training subsets), we did not find a measurable
benefit from quantum consistency modules: QLCM-R matches the classical
baseline's training loss and sample diversity while using 54\% more
parameters, and QLCM-H fails to train competitively and shows unstable,
seed-dependent mode collapse. Few-step (1/2/4-step) sampling quality is
stable for both placements once training performs genuine consistency
distillation from the teacher, resolving a degradation that persisted after
fixing only the sampler in isolation. These are negative results within the
scope tested, not evidence against quantum consistency modules in general.
Immediate next steps are: (1) test an EMA target network for consistency
distillation, as in the original formulation, in place of the current
online-student self-reference; (2) diagnose and address the persistent
Fashion-MNIST mode collapse, likely by increasing the training subset size or
student training budget; (3) test whether a larger teacher or latent
dimension changes the residual-placement result; and (4) extend the qubit
range to 8 qubits as originally planned, compute permitting.
